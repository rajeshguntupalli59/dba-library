Chapter 25: Data Replication

Data replication is the process of copying and synchronizing data from one database instance to another, and it sits at the heart of nearly every high-availability, disaster recovery, and scale-out architecture you will encounter in production. Whether you are maintaining a warm standby that can take over within seconds of a primary failure, distributing read traffic across multiple replicas, or synchronizing data between geographically separated data centers, understanding how replication works at a deep level is what separates a reactive DBA from a proactive one. This chapter covers the replication mechanisms available in SQL Server — Always On Availability Groups, transactional replication, and log shipping — and builds toward the real-world decisions — configuration choices, monitoring strategies, failure handling, and latency management — that determine whether your replication setup holds up under production load or quietly degrades until it fails you at the worst possible moment.

---

How Replication Works: Models, Terminology, and Trade-offs

Before touching a single configuration dialog or script, it helps to understand the landscape of replication models, because SQL Server offers multiple mechanisms, and choosing the wrong one for your workload can cost you months of rework.

Replication falls into two broad categories: physical replication and logical replication. Physical replication copies the raw byte-level changes from data files — it does not care what those bytes mean semantically, only that the replica ends up with identical disk pages as the primary. Logical replication, by contrast, copies higher-level change descriptions: which rows were inserted, updated, or deleted in which tables. Each model has a distinct set of strengths and constraints.

Log-based replication — the model behind Availability Groups — is byte-for-byte faithful. The replica matches the primary exactly, including indexes and system catalog entries, because both apply the same transaction log records. This makes replicas simple to reason about: you never worry about schema divergence. The downside is that a log-based replica cannot have a different schema and cannot replicate selectively. You replicate the whole database or nothing.

Row-based replication — SQL Server's transactional replication — offers far more flexibility. You can replicate a single table, filter rows, or feed heterogeneous subscribers. The trade-off is additional overhead and more opportunities for things to go wrong: schema changes on the publisher are not automatically propagated, and identity ranges need explicit management across publisher and subscribers.

There is also a distinction between synchronous and asynchronous replication. In synchronous mode, the primary does not acknowledge a commit to the application until at least one replica has confirmed it received (and possibly applied) the change. This eliminates data loss on primary failure but adds commit latency proportional to network round-trip time. In asynchronous mode, the primary commits immediately and replication happens in the background. Asynchronous replication is far more common in practice because the latency impact of synchronous replication is difficult to absorb in OLTP workloads.

A third concept worth establishing early is replication lag — the difference in time or transaction volume between what has been committed on the primary and what has been applied on the replica. Understanding, measuring, and controlling replication lag is one of the recurring themes throughout this chapter.

SQL Server approaches replication with a menu of named technologies, each with a distinct job. Always On Availability Groups are the high-availability and read-scale-out workhorse: whole databases, automatic failover, readable secondaries. Transactional Replication moves selected tables and filtered rows to subscribers with low latency — the reporting-feed and data-distribution tool. Merge Replication handles bidirectional sync for occasionally-connected clients. Snapshot Replication refreshes whole copies on a schedule. Log Shipping is the simple, robust DR primitive: scheduled log backups restored on a standby. Pick the mechanism that matches the problem; they compose poorly when forced into each other's roles.

How Availability Groups Move Data: Log Transport Under the Hood

An Availability Group looks like magic from the outside — write on the primary, read on the secondary — but underneath it is a log-shipping engine with precise, observable mechanics. Understanding those mechanics is what lets you diagnose lag, size secondaries, and predict failover behavior instead of guessing.

Everything starts with the transaction log. When a transaction commits on the primary, its log records are hardened to the primary's log disk first — durability is never outsourced. Then, in parallel, the primary ships log blocks (batches of log records, compressed in transit) to every secondary. Each secondary hardens the blocks to its own log disk and then redoes them — replaying the changes into its data files. Hardening and redo are separate stages, and that separation is the key to reading every replication metric correctly.

With synchronous commit, the primary waits until each synchronous secondary has hardened the log blocks before acknowledging the commit to the client. Note what it waits for: hardening, not redo. The secondary has the data durably; it just may not have applied it to data pages yet. With asynchronous commit, the primary ships the blocks and moves on — the secondary can fall arbitrarily far behind under a write burst, which is exactly what the send and redo queues measure.

Two queues tell the whole story, both visible in sys.dm_hadr_database_replica_states. The log send queue (log_send_queue_size) is log that the primary has hardened but not yet shipped — it grows when the network can't keep up or the secondary stops acknowledging. The redo queue (redo_queue_size) is log the secondary has received but not yet applied — it grows when the secondary's I/O or redo thread can't keep pace. A growing send queue points at the network or the primary; a growing redo queue points at the secondary. Diagnose accordingly.

sequenceDiagram\n    participant P as Primary\n    participant S1 as Sync secondary\n    participant S2 as Async secondary\n    P->>P: Transaction commits to local log\n    P->>S1: Ship log blocks\n    S1->>S1: Harden to log disk\n    S1-->>P: Acknowledge hardened\n    P->>P: Commit confirmed to client\n    P->>S2: Ship log blocks (async, no wait)\n    S2->>S2: Harden + redo

*Figure: AG log transport — the primary waits for a synchronous secondary to harden log blocks before confirming the commit, while an asynchronous secondary receives the same stream without blocking the primary.*

-- AG replication health: send and redo queues per database
SELECT
    ar.replica_server_name,
    dbrs.database_name,
    dbrs.synchronization_state_desc,
    dbrs.log_send_queue_size,
    dbrs.redo_queue_size,
    dbrs.estimated_data_loss_seconds
FROM sys.dm_hadr_database_replica_states dbrs
JOIN sys.availability_replicas ar
    ON dbrs.replica_id = ar.replica_id
ORDER BY dbrs.redo_queue_size DESC;

One more mechanic with outsized operational impact: the transaction log on the primary cannot be truncated past the oldest log needed by any secondary. If a secondary disconnects for a day, the primary's log grows for a day — check log_reuse_wait_desc and you will see AVAILABILITY_REPLICA staring back at you. This is by design (you asked for no data loss), but it means a dead secondary is a disk-full incident waiting to happen. Monitor it like one.

Always On Availability Groups in SQL Server

SQL Server's primary high-availability and disaster recovery mechanism for modern deployments is Always On Availability Groups, introduced in SQL Server 2012 and substantially improved through 2016, 2017, 2019, and 2022. An Availability Group (AG) is a container for one or more user databases that fail over together as a unit. The AG has a primary replica and one or more secondary replicas, with data synchronized via the transaction log, as described in the previous section.

AGs require Windows Server Failover Clustering (WSFC) in traditional deployments, though SQL Server 2017 introduced support for Linux and a "clusterless" configuration using the CLUSTER_TYPE = NONE option, which is useful for read-scale scenarios where automatic failover is not required. SQL Server 2022 further improved cross-platform flexibility.

Creating an AG involves several steps: enabling the AG feature on each SQL Server instance, creating the databases on the primary, taking full and log backups, restoring them to the secondaries with NORECOVERY, and then joining them to the AG. The AG listener — a virtual network name and IP address — provides a single connection endpoint that redirects clients to the current primary.

Each secondary can be configured independently as synchronous or asynchronous and as readable or not readable. A readable secondary allows reporting queries and backups to be offloaded from the primary, which is one of the most compelling operational features of AGs. The readable secondary is an eventually-consistent read-only view of the primary's data, with snapshot isolation handling the concurrency.

-- SQL Server: Check AG health and failover readiness
SELECT
    ag.name AS ag_name,
    ar.replica_server_name,
    ar.availability_mode_desc,
    ar.failover_mode_desc,
    ars.role_desc,
    ars.operational_state_desc,
    ars.connected_state_desc,
    ars.synchronization_health_desc,
    ars.last_connect_error_description
FROM sys.availability_groups ag
JOIN sys.availability_replicas ar
    ON ag.group_id = ar.group_id
JOIN sys.dm_hadr_availability_replica_states ars
    ON ar.replica_id = ars.replica_id;

Failover behavior is a major consideration. In an AG with synchronous replicas and WSFC-based automatic failover configured, SQL Server can fail over to a synchronous secondary automatically when the primary becomes unavailable, with zero data loss guaranteed by definition of synchronous replication. Asynchronous replicas can only be used for manual failover, and because they may lag behind, a forced failover may involve data loss. SQL Server quantifies this with the recovery point objective (RPO) metric visible in the DMVs — the amount of committed transaction log that has not yet been hardened on the secondary.

One area where AG configuration frequently trips up teams is the impact of synchronous secondaries on primary throughput. Every commit on the primary must wait for acknowledgment from all synchronous secondaries. In environments with cross-datacenter replication where network latency is 5–20 ms, synchronous commit can cut OLTP throughput dramatically. A common production pattern is to have synchronous replication within a datacenter for automatic failover and asynchronous replication to a distant DR site, accepting potential data loss for the DR replica in exchange for no performance impact on the primary.

Logical Replication: Selective, Flexible, and Version-Aware

Transactional replication addresses use cases that Availability Groups cannot — replicating a subset of tables, filtering rows per subscriber, bridging versions during an upgrade, and feeding reporting servers or heterogeneous subscribers. Where an AG replicates whole databases for availability, transactional replication distributes selected data for consumption.

Transactional replication uses a publisher / distributor / subscriber model. The publisher defines articles — tables or filtered row subsets — grouped into a publication. The distributor (often co-located with the publisher) stores the changes; subscribers consume them. Two agents do the work: the Log Reader Agent harvests committed transactions from the publication database's transaction log, and the Distribution Agent applies them to each subscriber.

-- SQL Server: Create a transactional publication (via T-SQL)
USE [sales];
EXEC sp_replicationdboption
    @dbname = N'sales',
    @optname = N'publish',
    @value = N'true';

EXEC sp_addpublication
    @publication = N'sales_pub',
    @status = N'active',
    @sync_method = N'concurrent',
    @repl_freq = N'continuous';

EXEC sp_addarticle
    @publication = N'sales_pub',
    @article = N'orders',
    @source_object = N'orders',
    @type = N'logbased';

The Log Reader Agent scans the publication database's transaction log for marked transactions and writes them to the distribution database — it reads the log rather than using triggers, so the overhead on the publisher is modest. But the log cannot be truncated past the reader's position: a stalled Log Reader Agent grows the publisher's log, exactly like an AG secondary that stops receiving. Monitor the agent's latency and the log's reuse wait as a pair.

A key limitation to understand: schema changes are not replicated by default. If you add a column on the publisher, apply it with sp_repladdcolumn / sp_repldropcolumn or enable schema-change replication on the publication — otherwise the subscriber's schema drifts and the Distribution Agent errors out on the first row referencing the new column. This is the fundamental operational difference from AGs, where DDL is just more log and replicates automatically.

Sequences are another gap. Because sequences generate values independently on each instance, NEXTVAL calls on the publisher do not update the sequence on the subscriber. After a failover or migration using logical replication, sequence positions on the new primary must be manually advanced past any values that were used by the old primary.

SQL Server's Transactional Replication also reads from the transaction log but does so through the Log Reader Agent, a component that runs as a SQL Server Agent job. The Log Reader scans the publisher's transaction log for changes marked for replication and writes them to the distribution database — a staging database that holds the pending changes. The Distribution Agent then reads from the distribution database and applies changes to the subscriber. This three-tier architecture (publisher → distributor → subscriber) gives SQL Server replication more flexibility in routing and scheduling but adds more moving parts to monitor.

Merge Replication handles bidirectional synchronization where both publisher and subscriber can make changes that need to be reconciled. This is powerful for occasionally-connected scenarios like mobile or branch office databases, but conflict resolution — what happens when the same row is updated differently on both sides — is notoriously complex to configure correctly and debug. Most modern architectures avoid Merge Replication in favor of application-level conflict handling or eventual-consistency designs.

Monitoring Replication Health and Managing Lag in Production

A replication topology that is not actively monitored is a topology that will surprise you during an incident. The operational reality of replication is that lag accumulates gradually under high load, replica connections drop due to network blips, and the send queue accumulates on a stalled secondary — all silently, unless you have built the observability layer.

The fundamental metric to watch is replication lag. For AGs, sys.dm_hadr_database_replica_states exposes log_send_queue_size (log the primary hasn't shipped), redo_queue_size (log the secondary hasn't applied), and estimated_data_loss_seconds for async replicas. Time-based alerting on redo-queue growth catches a struggling secondary before a failover becomes unsafe — a secondary with a ten-minute redo queue is ten minutes of potential data loss away from being a good failover target.

-- Monitor AG lag and estimated data loss window
SELECT
    ar.replica_server_name,
    adc.database_name,
    drs.synchronization_state_desc,
    drs.log_send_queue_size AS unsent_log_kb,
    drs.redo_queue_size AS unapplied_log_kb,
    DATEDIFF(SECOND, drs.last_redone_time, GETUTCDATE()) AS seconds_since_last_redo,
    ar.availability_mode_desc,
    drs.estimated_data_loss_seconds
FROM sys.dm_hadr_database_replica_states drs
JOIN sys.availability_replicas ar
    ON drs.replica_id = ar.replica_id
JOIN sys.availability_databases_cluster adc
    ON drs.group_database_id = adc.group_database_id
WHERE drs.is_local = 0
ORDER BY drs.redo_queue_size DESC;

Lag spikes almost always have one of three causes. The first is a sudden burst of writes on the primary — a large batch job, a bulk load, or a runaway update — that temporarily outpaces the replication throughput. The second is I/O saturation on the standby, where the secondary's disk cannot keep up with hardening the incoming log blocks. The third is network bandwidth exhaustion between primary and standby, particularly relevant for cross-datacenter replication over a saturated link.

AG log transport compresses log blocks in flight, so cross-datacenter bandwidth is rarely the bottleneck — but the send queue still grows when a write burst outpaces the link. When it does, the fix is reducing the burst (batch the load) or increasing the pipe, not a compression knob: there isn't one to turn.

In SQL Server, the equivalent concern is log send queue growth. The log_send_rate from dm_hadr_database_replica_states tells you how fast log is being shipped. If log_send_queue_size is growing faster than log_send_rate can drain it, you have a sending bottleneck — either network bandwidth, or the secondary's redo rate is causing backpressure.

For proactive alerting, a production-grade setup should alert on:

  • → Replication lag (redo queue growth / estimated data loss) exceeding thresholds — alert at >30s, page at >5min is a reasonable starting point
  • → Log send queues growing without bound — a secondary that stops acknowledging holds the primary's log from truncating; alert on log_send_queue_size and on log_reuse_wait_desc = 'AVAILABILITY_REPLICA'
  • → Any standby that has been disconnected for more than a configurable window
  • → In SQL Server, any AG secondary whose synchronization health is not HEALTHY

    • For trend analysis, ship the AG DMV metrics into your monitoring system — the Prometheus sql_exporter, Telegraf, or SCOM all expose them. A redo-queue spike that lasts two minutes during a nightly batch run is normal and acceptable; a spike that lasts two minutes every hour at random is a symptom that needs investigation.

    Planned maintenance on an AG secondary — patching the OS, upgrading SQL Server — is straightforward: if it's the primary, fail over first; do the work; fail back. Rolling through the replicas this way gives you zero-downtime patching, and the secondary simply resumes log transport where it paused when it rejoins.

    Failover, Promotion, and Post-Failover Operations

    Replication topology is only as valuable as your ability to act on it when the primary fails. The failover process — promoting a standby to primary — looks straightforward on paper but involves enough edge cases and post-promotion tasks that it deserves careful planning and regular testing.

    A planned manual failover is ALTER AVAILABILITY GROUP [ag] FAILOVER — the primary and a synchronous secondary coordinate so no committed data is lost, and the AG listener follows the role change. Automatic failover does the same without human intervention when the cluster detects a primary failure and quorum agrees.

    Forced failover — allowing data loss — is the disaster option: the secondary comes online without the primary's cooperation, and any log that never arrived is gone. The old primary must never accept writes afterward; the cluster enforces this by removing it from membership, because a resurrected old primary taking writes is the classic split-brain. After a forced failover, the old primary cannot simply rejoin as a secondary — its log chain diverged, so it must be re-seeded (automatic seeding or backup/restore) before rejoining the AG.

    Automatic seeding handles the common case: when a replica rejoins after a divergence, SQL Server streams the database across and rebuilds it. For very large databases or slow links, seed from a backup instead — restore WITH NORECOVERY on the secondary and join it to the AG; log transport takes it from there.

    In SQL Server Always On, failover is more formalized. A planned manual failover to a synchronous secondary involves connecting to the primary, verifying the secondary is synchronized, and executing the failover command:

    -- SQL Server: Initiate a planned manual failover to a synchronous secondary
-- (Connect to the secondary you want to promote)
ALTER AVAILABILITY GROUP [SalesAG] FAILOVER;
    -- Planned manual failover of an availability group (no data loss)
-- Run on the intended new primary (a synchronous secondary)
ALTER AVAILABILITY GROUP [ProductionAG] FAILOVER;

-- Forced failover after a primary disaster (allows data loss)
-- ALTER AVAILABILITY GROUP [ProductionAG] FORCE_FAILOVER_ALLOW_DATA_LOSS;

    A forced failover in SQL Server — used when the primary is unavailable — requires the WITH DATA_LOSS clause and should only be used with full understanding that any unconfirmed transactions from the old primary will be lost:

    -- SQL Server: Force failover to an asynchronous secondary (accepts potential data loss)
-- (Connect to the target secondary)
ALTER AVAILABILITY GROUP [SalesAG] FORCE_FAILOVER_ALLOW_DATA_LOSS;

    After any failover, whether planned or forced, a checklist of post-failover tasks must be executed before the new topology can be considered stable.

    The post-failover checklist includes:

    1. Verify the listener follows — with the AG listener this is normally automatic, but direct-connect strings, linked servers, and monitoring agents that bypass the listener must point at the new primary.

    2. Re-seed the old primary — after a forced failover, rejoin it as a secondary via automatic seeding or a restored backup before trusting the topology again.

    3. Advance sequences — if the old primary had sequences that outpaced the replica's last-replicated values, set them forward manually on the new primary to avoid duplicate key errors.

    4. Drop and recreate replication slots — slots from the old primary are invalid. Any logical replication subscribers that were connected to the old primary need their subscriptions refreshed against the new primary.

    5. Verify log backups — ensure transaction log backup jobs are running against the new primary; a failover that nobody told the backup jobs about is how you discover gaps in the log chain during the next restore drill.

    6. Update monitoring and alerting — any metrics pointing at the old primary hostname need to be redirected.

    For SQL Server, the post-failover checklist adds:

    1. Reconnect the old primary as a secondary — once the old primary is recovered, add it back to the AG as a secondary replica and allow it to resynchronize.

    2. Transfer AG-dependent jobs — SQL Server Agent jobs that were running on the primary do not automatically transfer. Jobs need to be enabled on the new primary if they are designed to run only on the current primary.

    3. Verify linked server connections — linked servers that depended on the old primary's hostname or listener may need to be reconfigured if using a non-listener connection string.

    4. Check AG listener DNS TTL — client applications connecting via the AG listener should reconnect automatically, but clients that cache DNS may briefly connect to the old primary. Ensure listener DNS TTL is low enough to resolve quickly.

    5. Audit data loss — if a forced failover was performed, use the transaction log backup chain on the old primary to determine exactly which transactions were lost and notify application owners if any business data was affected.

    The most important operational practice for failover is regular testing. A failover procedure that has never been rehearsed in production-like conditions is not a failover procedure — it is a hypothesis. Scheduled failover tests, sometimes called "chaos" exercises, force you to discover gaps in connection string management, session handling, queue-backed services that need reconnection logic, and all the other dependencies that are invisible until the primary disappears.

    Replication and Schema Changes

    Schema changes in a replicated environment deserve their own section because they are the source of a disproportionate number of replication outages. The behavior of DDL under replication is fundamentally different between Availability Groups and transactional replication.

    Under Availability Groups, DDL is fully replicated — AGs ship transaction log records, and DDL is logged just like DML. You can run ALTER TABLE, CREATE INDEX (including ONLINE), or DROP TABLE on the primary and it propagates to all secondaries automatically. There is no special handling required. The only subtlety is that long-running DDL holds schema locks that also block redo on the secondaries — a big index build can stall readable-secondary queries until it finishes.

    Under transactional replication, DDL is not replicated by default. The subscriber maintains its own schema, so apply schema changes with sp_repladdcolumn / sp_repldropcolumn or enable schema-change replication on the publication — before any DML arrives that references the new schema. The safe sequence for an additive change (adding a column) is:

    1. Apply the ALTER TABLE ... ADD COLUMN on the subscriber first.

    2. Apply the same change on the publisher.

    3. Verify the subscription is still running and applying changes.

    For destructive changes (dropping a column), reverse the order: drop on the publisher first, verify the replication stream is draining, then drop on the subscriber.

    In SQL Server Transactional Replication, the behavior depends on how the publication is configured. By default, schema changes on the publisher are propagated to subscribers, and SQL Server has a dedicated mechanism — the Schema Change event — that the Log Reader detects and forwards through the distribution database. However, not all DDL is supported for automatic propagation, and you can disable schema change replication if your subscriber has a different schema intentionally. The @replicate_ddl parameter on sp_addpublication controls this.

    -- SQL Server: Check if schema changes are being replicated for a publication
SELECT
    publication,
    replicate_ddl,
    sync_method,
    status
FROM syspublications
WHERE name = 'sales_pub';
    -- Post a tracer token to measure end-to-end replication latency
DECLARE @token INT;
EXEC sp_posttracertoken
    @publication = N'MyPublication',
    @tracer_token_id = @token OUTPUT;

-- Read back how long the token took through each agent
EXEC sp_helptracertokenhistory
    @publication = N'MyPublication',
    @tracer_id = @token;

    The general principle across all replication types: schema changes need a plan that accounts for where the change must be applied, in what order, and what happens to in-flight replication if a mismatch exists even briefly. For zero-downtime deployments, expand-contract patterns work well — add the new column as nullable first, backfill it, make it non-nullable or add the constraint, and only then remove the old column — because each individual step can be replicated safely while the application continues running.

    Cascading Replication and Fan-Out Topologies

    Not all replication topologies are simple primary-to-single-standby configurations. Production environments frequently need cascading replication — where a standby itself serves as the source for additional standbys downstream — or fan-out configurations where a single primary sends to many replicas simultaneously.

    AGs do not cascade: a secondary cannot forward log blocks to another secondary. For fan-out across sites, use a distributed availability group — an AG of AGs, where a forwarder secondary on the remote site receives log blocks and feeds the remote AG's primary, which then fans out locally. One transcontinental stream instead of one per replica.

    -- On the distributed AG forwarder: confirm log is flowing to the remote AG
SELECT
    ar.replica_server_name,
    dbrs.database_name,
    dbrs.synchronization_state_desc,
    dbrs.log_send_queue_size,
    dbrs.redo_queue_size
FROM sys.dm_hadr_database_replica_states dbrs
JOIN sys.availability_replicas ar
    ON dbrs.replica_id = ar.replica_id
WHERE ar.replica_server_name = N'RemoteForwarder';

    In SQL Server, Always On AGs do not natively support cascading in the same sense, but Distributed Availability Groups (introduced in SQL Server 2016) allow one AG to act as the primary data source for a second AG in a different WSFC cluster or on Linux. This enables cross-cluster, cross-region topologies where the two AGs are independently manageable but synchronized through a global primary-to-global secondary relationship.

    Fan-out topologies — where many subscribers consume from a single publisher — are straightforward in transactional replication, and subscribers can republish to build a tree. The concern with large fan-out is that each subscriber adds a Distribution Agent and another copy of the data to keep current. Keep trees shallow: every level adds latency and another distribution database to monitor.

    Replication for Zero-Downtime Upgrades

    One of the most practically valuable patterns is the rolling upgrade of an Availability Group. Patch the secondaries first, fail over, then patch the old primary: zero downtime, zero data movement, and the AG keeps serving throughout.

    The procedure in outline:

    1. For a major version upgrade (e.g., SQL Server 2019 to 2022), build the new-version replicas alongside the existing AG and join them with a distributed availability group — the AG protocol supports mixed versions during the migration window.

    2. Seed the new replicas from a backup (restore WITH NORECOVERY) or automatic seeding, then let log transport keep them current until cutover.

    3. Fail over to the new version, verify the workload, then decommission the old side. Transactional replication can bridge the same gap for databases that can't take an AG: replicate from the old publisher to the new subscriber and cut over when lag is near zero.

    4. Create a subscription on the new instance pointing at the publication on the old instance.

    5. Allow the subscription to synchronize fully — the initial table sync copies all existing data, then streaming logical replication keeps the new instance current.

    6. Monitor replication lag until it is near zero.

    7. During a brief maintenance window, stop writes to the old instance, verify the new instance has consumed all remaining changes, advance sequences on the new instance, and redirect application traffic.

    SQL Server handles major version upgrades differently, using rolling upgrades within an AG. The procedure involves upgrading secondary replicas to the new SQL Server version first (while the primary remains on the old version), then failing over to an upgraded secondary, and finally upgrading the former primary. This is supported because SQL Server AG replication is backward-compatible for one major version during the upgrade window. The online documentation for each SQL Server release specifies the exact supported rolling upgrade path.

    Handling Split-Brain and Network Partitions

    The nightmare scenario in any replication topology is split-brain: two nodes both believing they are the primary, both accepting writes, with no mechanism to reconcile the divergence. Split-brain is not merely a theoretical concern — it happens in real production environments due to network partitions, misconfigured fencing, and automation bugs.

    AGs never fail over on replication state alone — failover decisions belong to the Windows Server Failover Cluster. Automatic failover requires synchronous commit plus cluster quorum; without quorum, no failover happens, which is the safe direction to fail. Manual failover during a real outage is how split-brain starts: forcing a secondary online while the old primary is still reachable by clients.

    The cluster's equivalent of fencing: when a node loses quorum or fails its health check, the cluster evicts it from membership and the AG role moves. A node that cannot see quorum cannot become primary — this is what prevents split-brain during a network partition. The dangerous window is the one you create yourself with forced failover; treat FORCE_FAILOVER_ALLOW_DATA_LOSS as the break-glass it is, and re-establish quorum before trusting the topology again.

    In SQL Server with WSFC-based Availability Groups, split-brain is prevented by the quorum mechanism: the cluster requires a majority of quorum votes to remain operational, so a network partition that isolates a subset of nodes will cause that subset to lose quorum and stop accepting writes. The cluster configuration must be designed so that no minority partition can independently achieve quorum — this typically involves careful placement of the File Share Witness or Cloud Witness in a third failure domain.

    -- SQL Server: Check WSFC quorum state and node votes
SELECT
    member_name,
    member_type_desc,
    member_state_desc,
    number_of_quorum_votes
FROM sys.dm_hadr_cluster_members;

-- Check if current instance is part of the quorum-capable majority
SELECT
    cluster_name,
    quorum_type_desc,
    quorum_state_desc
FROM sys.dm_hadr_cluster;
    -- Check what role each replica currently holds and its health
SELECT
    ar.replica_server_name,
    ars.role_desc,
    ars.operational_state_desc,
    ars.connected_state_desc
FROM sys.dm_hadr_availability_replica_states ars
JOIN sys.availability_replicas ar
    ON ars.replica_id = ar.replica_id;

    The engineering principle underlying all split-brain prevention is the same: no node should be able to promote itself to primary without first confirming that no other node currently holds the primary role, and that confirmation must come from a quorum or consensus mechanism that is itself partition-tolerant.

    Key Takeaways

  • → **Log-based replication** (Availability Groups) ships transaction log records and produces identical replicas automatically, including DDL — but every replica shares the database's schema and version family. **Row-based replication** (transactional replication) replicates selectively and can bridge versions, but requires explicit DDL management and has no automatic failover.
  • → **Replication lag** is the central operational metric for any replicated environment. Monitor log_send_queue_size, redo_queue_size, and estimated_data_loss_seconds; alert on thresholds appropriate for your RPO; and investigate systematically — spikes trace to write bursts, secondary I/O saturation, or network bandwidth constraints.
  • → **A stalled secondary holds the primary's log hostage.** Whether it's an AG secondary that stopped receiving or a Log Reader Agent that stopped reading, log_reuse_wait_desc tells you who is blocking truncation. Alert on it — an unmonitored stall fills the log disk.
  • → **Failover is not complete at the moment of promotion.** Post-failover tasks — redirecting applications, reconfiguring or rebuilding old standbys, advancing sequences, transferring SQL Server Agent jobs, verifying AG listeners — are as important as the promotion itself. The only way to know your failover procedure is reliable is to test it regularly under realistic conditions.
  • → **Split-brain prevention requires quorum, not just replication.** AG automatic failover depends on WSFC quorum — understand what happens to failover during a partial network partition before it happens to you in production.
