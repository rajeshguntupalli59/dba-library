On-call as a SQL Server DBA: surviving the 2 AM page without burning out

Rajesh Guntupalli·November 2026·6 min read

My phone went off at 2:14 AM. "Production database unreachable." I was awake, heart pounding, staring at a laptop in the dark — and I had no idea where to start. I spent forty minutes poking around before I found the real problem: a log file that had filled the disk, which I could have checked in ten seconds.

That night taught me the truth about on-call: the hard part is never the database. The hard part is being a tired human trying to think clearly under pressure. Everything below is what I wish someone had handed me before my first rotation.

---

1. Build your 2 AM toolkit before you need it

At 2 AM you cannot write good T-SQL. You can only run T-SQL you already wrote. Keep a single script file — your triage kit — with the five queries you will always reach for first, and practice running them on your lab server until they are muscle memory.

The first query answers the only question that matters at the start: what is running right now, and what is it waiting on?

-- GOOD: one query that tells you what is running and what it waits on
SELECT
    r.session_id,
    r.status,
    r.wait_type,
    r.wait_time / 1000 AS wait_seconds,
    r.cpu_time,
    r.logical_reads,
    LEFT(t.text, 120) AS sql_text
FROM sys.dm_exec_requests r
CROSS APPLY sys.dm_exec_sql_text(r.sql_handle) t
WHERE r.session_id > 50
ORDER BY r.wait_time DESC;

Contrast that with what most of us do when we are panicking:

-- BAD: guessing in the dark
EXEC sp_who2;                       -- 200 rows, no wait info, no SQL text
SELECT * FROM sys.dm_exec_requests; -- raw DMVs with no filtering or joins

Your kit should also hold a blocking-chain query, a log-space check (DBCC SQLPERF(LOGSPACE)), a disk-space check, and an Availability Group health query. Five queries, saved, tested, ready. The night you need them, you will not remember syntax — you will only remember where the file is.

Keep the kit in source control, not on one laptop. The night your laptop is in another room and you are on a borrowed machine, a shared repo link is the difference between triaging in two minutes and rebuilding your toolkit from memory. Review it once a quarter: a query that worked on SQL Server 2019 may need a tweak after an upgrade, and you do not want to discover that mid-incident.

---

2. Triage in order: down, slow, or lying?

Every page falls into one of three buckets, and the bucket decides your next move. Work them in order and do not skip ahead.

Down: the instance is unreachable or a database is offline. Check the service, check the error log, check disk space. Nothing else matters until the instance answers. SELECT name, state_desc FROM sys.databases tells you in one line whether a database is actually offline or just slow.

Slow: the instance is up but everything crawls. Run your triage query from section 1. If one session is blocking fifty others, you have found your head blocker — deal with it, do not start tuning random queries. If every session waits on the same thing, that wait type is your answer.

Lying: the alert fired but nothing is actually wrong. A threshold was too tight, a job ran long once, a replica lagged during a backup. Confirm with your own eyes, then silence the alert properly — do not just acknowledge it and let it fire again tomorrow night.

The classic beginner mistake is treating a "slow" page like a "down" page and restarting the instance. A restart clears the plan cache, empties the buffer pool, and turns a ten-minute problem into a forty-minute one. Restart last, never first.

---

3. Write the runbook while the incident is still fresh

The most valuable thing you produce during an incident is not the fix. It is the note you write in the twenty minutes after the fix, while you still remember what confused you.

A good runbook entry has four lines: what the alert said, what the real cause was, the exact commands you ran, and what would have caught it earlier. That is it. Nobody reads a ten-page postmortem at 2 AM; everybody reads four lines.

Here is the part most teams skip: the next time that alert fires, the person on call should not have to think. They should follow the runbook, run the commands, and either fix it or escalate with the evidence already in hand. Every incident that repeats without a runbook is an incident your team chose to suffer twice.

Keep the runbook where the pager can reach it — in the alert itself, or one link away. A runbook buried in a wiki nobody opens is the same as no runbook.

---

4. Fix the alert, not just the incident

After every page, ask one question: should this alert have fired at all? On-call burnout is rarely caused by real emergencies. It is caused by alerts that fire when nothing is wrong, at thresholds nobody tuned, for conditions nobody can act on.

Keep a simple rule: an alert that pages a human must be actionable, urgent, and real. If it is not actionable, make it a ticket. If it is not urgent, make it a daytime notification. If it is not real, fix the threshold or delete the alert. I have deleted more alerts than I have created, and on-call got better every single time.

The same discipline applies to the fix itself. The 2 AM fix is allowed to be ugly — a killed session, a grown log file, a failed-over replica. But the ugly fix must come with a daytime follow-up: a ticket, an owner, and a date. Otherwise the same page wakes someone up again next month, and the team quietly learns that on-call is just suffering with extra steps.

---

5. Protect the human holding the pager

This is the section nobody puts in a runbook, and it is the one that decides whether you last two years or ten.

First, rotation is not optional. One person permanently on call is not dedication, it is a resignation waiting to happen. If your team is too small for a real rotation, say so out loud and negotiate cover — even a buddy system where someone else holds the pager one weekend a month changes everything.

Second, protect your sleep like it is production. Phone on loud, laptop charged, triage kit open in a tab before bed. The thirty seconds you save not fumbling for a VPN password at 2 AM are thirty seconds of clear thinking you get back.

Third, escalate early and without shame. Calling a second person at 2:30 AM is not failure — it is the job. The incidents that become disasters are almost always the ones where someone sat alone for two hours, too proud to wake anyone up.

And finally, the morning after a bad night, take the recovery time. A DBA who worked until 4 AM and is expected at standup at 9 AM will make the next outage worse, not better. If your team does not have a rule for this, propose one. You are not asking for a favor; you are protecting the next incident.

---

Closing Thoughts

Good on-call is boring on purpose. The toolkit is written ahead of time, the triage order is decided ahead of time, the runbook is waiting, and the alerts that fire are the ones worth waking up for. The drama is not the job — the preparation is the job.

None of this requires a big team or expensive tooling. A shared script file, a four-line runbook habit, and a rotation you actually honor will do more for your nights than any monitoring dashboard ever will.

If you take one thing from this post, take the triage kit. Write those five queries this week, run them against your lab, and save them somewhere you can reach half-asleep. The next 2 AM page will still wake you up. It just will not scare you.
