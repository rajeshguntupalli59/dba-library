Text-to-SQL in production: what actually works in 2026

Rajesh Guntupalli·August 2026·5 min read

"Let the business team ask the database questions in plain English" has been the demo everyone builds and almost nobody ships correctly. I've now put text-to-SQL in front of real users at two companies, and the gap between the demo and the production system is entirely about what happens when the model is wrong — not how often it's right.

Model quality stopped being the bottleneck a while ago. Claude and GPT-class models write syntactically correct, schema-aware SQL from natural language with genuinely impressive accuracy against a well-documented schema. The problem is that "impressive accuracy" still means wrong often enough that an unguarded system will eventually tell someone the wrong revenue number with complete confidence, and that one incident costs you more trust than a hundred correct answers earned.

Where it works well

Ad hoc exploration against a well-modeled, well-documented schema is the sweet spot. If your table and column names are self-explanatory, foreign keys are declared (not just implied), and you feed the model a current schema description rather than trusting its memory of your codebase from a training cutoff, you get genuinely useful queries for the kind of question that used to mean pinging a data analyst and waiting a day.

-- "How many orders over $500 did we get last week, by state?"
SELECT s.state, COUNT(*) AS order_count, SUM(o.total) AS revenue
FROM orders o
JOIN shipping_addresses s ON s.order_id = o.id
WHERE o.total > 500
  AND o.created_at >= NOW() - INTERVAL '7 days'
GROUP BY s.state
ORDER BY revenue DESC;

That query is correct, idiomatic, and exactly what a competent analyst would write. Feeding the model your actual information_schema output, real column comments, and a handful of example query/answer pairs for your specific schema's quirks (which column actually holds "active" status, which of your three created_at-looking columns is the one that matters) makes a bigger accuracy difference than switching models.

Where it quietly fails

The failures that matter aren't syntax errors — those are obvious and get caught immediately. The dangerous ones are queries that run successfully and return a plausible, wrong number. Ambiguous business logic is the biggest source: "active customers" might mean status = 'active', or it might mean "placed an order in the last 90 days," and the model will confidently pick one without flagging the ambiguity unless you've told it the definition explicitly.

Joins across tables with similar-looking columns are the second big source. A schema with both orders.customer_id and orders.billed_customer_id (one for the account, one for who actually paid, because of a gift-purchase edge case someone added two years ago) is exactly the kind of tribal knowledge a model has no way to know unless it's documented somewhere the model can read.

The guardrails that make it shippable

Run generated SQL through a read-only role with a strict statement timeout and row cap, the same way I'd configure any AI agent's database access — never let generated SQL touch a write path. Beyond that, three things separated the systems that stayed trustworthy from the ones that quietly eroded user confidence:

First, show the generated SQL, always, not just the result. Users who can see WHERE status = 'active' and know that's not what they meant will say so. Users who only see a number have no way to catch the error, and neither do you until someone makes a decision based on it.

Second, maintain a small library of verified query patterns for your genuinely ambiguous business terms, and inject the relevant ones into context based on keyword matching before generation. This is unglamorous and it's the single highest-leverage thing I've done to cut wrong-answer rate — it turns "the model guessed" into "the model looked up the answer your team already agreed on."

Third, log every generated query alongside the natural-language question that produced it. Review that log weekly, early on. The patterns of what confuses the model are specific to your schema and your users' phrasing, and you'll find them faster by reading real queries than by theorizing.

Text-to-SQL earned its place in my toolkit this year — not as a replacement for a data team, but as a genuinely good first draft that a human glances at instead of writing from scratch. Ship it that way — visible SQL, bounded permissions, documented ambiguity — and it holds up. Ship it as a black box that just answers questions, and it will eventually answer one wrong at the worst possible moment.
