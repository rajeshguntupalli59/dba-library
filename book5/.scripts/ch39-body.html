Chapter 39: Right-Sizing Cloud Database Instances

Right-sizing is the highest-ROI activity in cloud database operations: the workload needs what it needs, and everything above that is waste billed by the hour. On-premises, oversizing was rational (hardware is bought once, and running out hurts more than overprovisioning). In the cloud, oversizing is a subscription to waste — and unlike hardware, it can be fixed this afternoon. This chapter builds the right-sizing practice for SQL Server: the measurement methodology, the resizing mechanics per platform, storage right-sizing, and the automation that keeps sizes honest as workloads evolve.

39.1 The Measurement Methodology: What "Right" Means

"Right" is defined by the workload's actual consumption with headroom for peaks — not by the on-premises server it replaced, not by the vendor's recommendation, and not by anyone's comfort. The methodology: collect 4–8 weeks of metrics (CPU utilization, memory pressure signals, IOPS and throughput, connection counts — covering a full business cycle including month/quarter-end); compute the p95 and peak for each dimension (averages lie — size for the peaks you must absorb, not the average you usually see); and define the headroom policy (typically 30–50% above p95 for production — the buffer that absorbs growth and spikes without paging anyone). The output is a sizing envelope per database: "needs 6–8 vCores at peak, 32 GB memory working set, 3,000 IOPS p95" — a specification the instance selection answers, not a guess.

39.2 Identifying Sizing Mismatches from Cloud Metrics

The mismatch signatures: chronically low CPU (p95 below 30% — the instance is at least one size too large); memory pressure without CPU pressure (low page life expectancy, RESOURCE_SEMAPHORE waits — needs a memory-optimized shape, not a bigger general-purpose one); IOPS throttling with idle CPU (storage-bound — fix the storage tier per Chapter 36, not the instance size); CPU-saturated with memory to spare (compute-optimized shape or scale-out, not a memory upgrade). SQL Server-specific signals: sys.dm_os_wait_stats dominated by CXPACKET (too many schedulers for the workload — smaller or MAXDOP tuning beats upsizing); persistent SOS_SCHEDULER_YIELD (genuinely CPU-bound — upsize or scale out). Run the mismatch scan fleet-wide monthly — it takes an hour and routinely finds 20–30% waste.

39.3 Vertical Scaling: Resizing Mechanics per Platform

The resize operation per platform: RDS — modify the instance class (minutes of downtime for Single-AZ; Multi-AZ resizes with a failover — plan the timing); Azure SQL Database/MI — change vCores or service tier online (the platform handles it — the closest to "free" resizing in the industry); EC2/Azure VMs/GCE — stop, resize the VM, start (downtime measured in minutes — schedule it); Cloud SQL — update machine type (brief downtime). The practice: resize during the maintenance window, verify the workload's key queries after (Query Store comparison — a smaller instance changes plan choices), and keep the previous size documented for fast rollback. Downsize in steps (one size at a time, observe a full business cycle) — the aggressive two-size downsize that causes a Monday-morning incident erases a year of savings goodwill.

39.4 Storage Right-Sizing: IOPS, Throughput, and Growth

Storage is the forgotten half of right-sizing: provisioned IOPS sized to measured p95 (not to the instance default — io2 volumes provisioned "to be safe" are a major waste line); throughput matched to the workload's actual MB/s (gp3's independent throughput provisioning exists precisely for this); allocated size with growth runway (storage only grows on most platforms — the one-time data load that permanently raised the bill from Chapter 19's warning); and tier placement (data on the performance tier, backups and cold data on cheaper tiers — Chapter 36's discipline). Monitor free space trends with alerts at 70% and 85% — the storage-full outage is the most embarrassing preventable incident, and autoscaling (where available) is the safety net, not the strategy.

39.5 Horizontal Scaling and Secondary Sizing

When vertical scaling hits limits (or economics), scale out per Chapter 37: readable secondaries sized to the read workload (not cloned from the primary), elastic pools for the multi-tenant fleets (Chapter 14), and Hyperscale for the workloads that outgrow provisioned tiers. The right-sizing lens on scale-out: every secondary gets the same measurement methodology as the primary (39.1) — secondaries sized by hope are the waste this chapter hunts. And revisit the scale-out decision itself annually: the workload that needed a secondary last year may fit on a current-generation primary this year (hardware improves; the architecture should be re-justified, not grandfathered).

39.6 Scheduled Scaling for Predictable Patterns

Not all workloads are flat — the 9-to-5 OLTP system, the nightly batch window, the weekend-quiet SaaS. Where the platform supports it (Azure SQL elastic jobs + automation, EC2 scheduled scaling, RDS scheduled actions), scale up for the known peak and down for the known trough: bigger during business hours, smaller overnight. The economics: a database at peak 12 hours a day on a smaller tier the other 12 saves roughly a quarter of compute — more than most reservation discounts, with zero commitment. The prerequisites: the scaling must be automated (manual scaling gets forgotten in exactly one direction — up), the application must tolerate the brief transitions, and the schedule must be reviewed when business patterns change (the "weekend quiet" that ended when the company went global).

39.7 Cost Allocation and Showback

Right-sizing sticks when the cost is visible to the owner: tag everything (application, team, environment, cost-center — the tagging discipline from Chapter 30); showback reports per team (your databases cost X, of which Y is identified waste with a recommended action); and the waste backlog (oversized instances as tracked work items with owners and dates — the same operational seriousness as the security backlog). The cultural point: waste is not blame — it's the default outcome of growth and caution. The showback report's job is to make the right size the easy choice, not to shame the team that provisioned generously during a launch.

39.8 Right-Sizing During Migration Waves

Migration is the best right-sizing opportunity you'll ever get (Chapter 33): the assessment phase produces the measurements, the target is being built fresh, and there's no "but it's always been that size" inertia. The practice: size the target from measured source metrics (not from the source server's spec — the 64-core on-premises box at 15% utilization becomes an 8-vCore cloud instance); start conservative-safe, then downsize (one size above the measurement for cutover safety, then the 39.3 downsize cadence on real cloud metrics); and revisit at 30/60/90 days post-migration (the migration-sized instance is a hypothesis — the 90-day review with production metrics is the verdict). Programs that skip this leave the migration's largest savings on the table.

Key Takeaways

  • → **Size from 4–8 weeks of measured p95/peak, plus 30–50% headroom.** The sizing envelope is a specification, not a guess. Averages lie; peaks define.
  • → **Read the mismatch signatures.** Low CPU = oversized; memory pressure = memory-optimized shape; IOPS throttling = storage tier; CXPACKET dominance = scheduler/parellelism tuning.
  • → **Downsize in steps, verify with Query Store.** One size at a time, a full business cycle of observation, documented rollback. Azure's online tier changes are the easiest; plan downtime for the rest.
  • → **Storage is half the bill — right-size it too.** Provisioned IOPS to measured p95, throughput matched, growth runway without bloat, tier placement by data temperature.
  • → **Automate the cadence: monthly mismatch scans, scheduled scaling, showback.** Migration waves are the best right-sizing opportunity — size targets from measurements, revisit at 90 days.
