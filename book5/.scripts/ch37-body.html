Chapter 37: Secondary and Scale-Out Economics: When Extra Copies Pay Off

Every secondary copy of a SQL Server database — a readable AG replica, a Business Critical secondary, a Hyperscale replica, a DR standby — costs real money: compute, storage, and (cross-region) data transfer. The question this chapter answers is economic, not architectural: when does the second (or third) copy pay for itself, and when is it an expensive insurance policy that never pays out? Note the SQL Server-specific framing: RDS for SQL Server has no read replicas, so "read replica economics" as other engines know it doesn't apply — the scale-out copies available to you are AG secondaries, platform replicas, and DR standbys, each with its own cost model.

37.2 The Real Cost Model: What You're Actually Paying For

A secondary's total cost has four components: compute (the replica's vCores/VM — often the largest line, and frequently oversized because secondaries get cloned from the primary's spec); storage (a full copy of the data — plus the IOPS tier, which is often provisioned to primary levels out of habit); licensing (self-managed secondaries need licenses unless covered by Software Assurance secondary rights — verify, because this is the line that surprises); and transfer (cross-AZ traffic is cheap or free; cross-region replication is metered — the distributed topologies from Chapters 16 and 25 pay this continuously). Model all four before adding a copy; the "free" readable secondary in Business Critical is free only because you already paid the tier premium.

37.3 When Secondaries Actually Pay Off

A readable secondary pays for itself when: reporting/analytical queries have a measurable cost on the primary (quantify it — the primary's CPU/IOPS consumed by reporting, translated to the instance size you'd need without offload); the secondary absorbs a workload that would otherwise force a primary upsizing (the classic win — a secondary at half the primary's size is cheaper than doubling the primary); or geographic read locality reduces latency enough to matter commercially. A secondary does not pay off when: the primary is idle (there's nothing to offload); the "reporting" is three queries a day (a scheduled extract serves them); or the secondary is sized identically to the primary "for safety" while serving 5% of its load — right-size the secondary to its actual workload (Chapter 39).

37.4 Provisioning and Routing: Getting the Architecture Right

The economics depend on correct routing: ApplicationIntent=ReadOnly connection strings (or the platform's read-scale endpoint) sending eligible traffic to secondaries automatically; workload classification (which queries are safe on a lagging secondary — the application must know its own staleness tolerance, Chapter 21); and lag-aware routing (if the secondary lags beyond tolerance, traffic returns to the primary — a secondary serving stale data to an intolerant application is a correctness incident, not a cost saving). The unrouted secondary — provisioned, paid for, receiving no traffic — is the most common waste in this chapter. Verify traffic split in monitoring, not in architecture diagrams.

37.5 Replica Lag: The Hidden Cost of Staleness

Lag has a price beyond correctness: application complexity (every read-after-write path needs primary routing — the code that forgets is the bug); support burden ("the dashboard shows yesterday's numbers" tickets); and the lag spike (secondaries lag most when the primary is busiest — exactly when offload matters most — so size secondaries for peak redo/apply throughput, not average). Monitor lag per secondary against the application's documented tolerance (Chapter 30); alert when tolerance is breached; and include lag behavior in the failover tests (Chapter 9) — the secondary you fail over to is the secondary your lag monitoring described.

37.6 The DR Standby: Insurance Economics

A DR standby that never serves traffic is pure insurance — price it as such: the standby's cost vs the cost of the outage it prevents (probability × impact — the business's numbers, not the DBA's); cheaper standby patterns (backup-based DR instead of a running standby where the RTO allows — Chapter 16's table; a stopped VM with attached storage; a smaller standby that scales up on failover); and the dual-use standby (a DR secondary that also serves reads converts insurance into infrastructure — the readable DR secondary is the best economics in this chapter when the RPO/RTO requirements allow async). The standby nobody can justify with numbers is the first thing cut in the next budget review — have the numbers ready before the review.

37.7 Cost Optimization for Secondary Fleets

The optimization checklist: right-size each secondary to its workload (not to the primary's spec); use the platform's included secondaries first (the BC readable secondary, Hyperscale replicas — already paid for); schedule what can be scheduled (a reporting secondary needed 8 hours a day doesn't run 24 — stop/start where the platform allows); commit to the stable secondaries (reserved/commitment pricing applies to secondary compute too — Chapter 38); and audit quarterly (secondaries accumulate — the "temporary" reporting replica from 18 months ago is still billing). Every secondary gets an owner, a purpose, and a review date; undocumented secondaries get decommissioned.

37.8 Choosing the Right Scale-Out Tool per Cloud

Per cloud, the economic ranking for read scale-out: AWS — readable AG secondaries on EC2 (full control, full cost) vs RDS (no read replicas — the platform gap that pushes read-heavy SQL Server workloads toward EC2 or toward Azure); Azure — the included BC secondary first, Hyperscale replicas for elastic scale, MI/VM AG secondaries for full control; GCP — GCE AG secondaries (Cloud SQL for SQL Server's replica story is thinner — verify current capabilities before committing). The meta-lesson: the cheapest read scale-out is the one your platform includes — and platform choice (Chapter 7) should have considered this workload from the start.

Key Takeaways

  • → **Model all four cost components: compute, storage, licensing, transfer.** Secondaries cloned from primary specs are usually oversized; licensing surprises are the most expensive.
  • → **Secondaries pay off by avoiding primary upsizing.** Quantify the offloaded load; verify traffic actually routes to the secondary; right-size to the read workload.
  • → **Lag is a correctness and cost parameter.** Document staleness tolerance, route lag-aware, size for peak apply throughput.
  • → **Price DR standbys as insurance — and prefer dual-use.** Backup-based DR where RTO allows; readable DR secondaries converting insurance into infrastructure.
  • → **Every secondary gets an owner, a purpose, and a review date.** Quarterly audits; use included platform secondaries first; commit to the stable ones.
