Chapter 36: Storage Tiers: gp2 vs gp3 vs io1 vs io2

Storage decisions that took minutes on-premises — "provision a 2TB LUN from the SAN, set stripe size to 64KB, done" — become architecture decisions in AWS RDS with lasting cost and performance consequences. The volume type you select at instance creation determines not just your baseline throughput and IOPS ceiling, but also how you scale, what you pay, and whether you can migrate away without downtime. This chapter breaks down the four RDS storage types — gp2, gp3, io1, and io2 — from the perspective of a DBA who already understands disk I/O mechanics and just needs to know how AWS maps those mechanics onto managed storage. You will learn when to use each tier, how to configure them correctly via CLI and Terraform, how to avoid the gp2 burst credit trap that degrades production performance silently, and how to make the right call for OLTP, analytics, and mixed workloads.

---

Understanding the Storage Landscape: What Replaced Your SAN

On-premises, you controlled every layer: disk spindle count, RAID level, controller cache, queue depth, stripe alignment. RDS abstracts all of that, but the underlying physics still apply. What AWS gives you is a set of pre-tuned abstractions backed by EBS (Elastic Block Store), and your job is to pick the right abstraction for your workload pattern.

The four volume types map loosely to familiar concepts:

| Volume Type | On-Premises Analog | Best For |

|---|---|---|

| gp2 | Mid-range SAN with burst tier | Dev, test, small OLTP |

| gp3 | Performance SAN with flat pricing | Most production workloads |

| io1 | All-flash array, dedicated IOPS | I/O-intensive OLTP |

| io2 | All-flash array, enterprise durability | Mission-critical, financial systems |

Key distinction before anything else: gp2 and gp3 are "general purpose" SSDs. io1 and io2 are "provisioned IOPS" SSDs. The naming matters because it controls what guarantees AWS makes about latency and IOPS consistency. General purpose volumes can throttle. Provisioned IOPS volumes guarantee sub-millisecond latency at the configured IOPS level.

The one fact that changes everything: gp2 ties IOPS to storage size. gp3 decouples them. This single architectural difference makes gp3 almost always the better default for new provisioning in 2024.

#### gp2: The Legacy Default You Should Stop Using

gp2 allocates 3 IOPS per GB of storage, with a minimum of 100 IOPS and a maximum of 16,000 IOPS. That ceiling hits at 5,334 GB (approximately 5.3 TB). Beyond that, you get no additional IOPS regardless of how much storage you add.

The burst mechanism works exactly like EC2 T-series CPU burst credits. Volumes under 1 TB accumulate burst credits allowing 3,000 IOPS for short periods. When credits are exhausted, performance drops to the baseline 3 IOPS/GB. A 100 GB gp2 volume has a baseline of 300 IOPS. During credit burst it can hit 3,000 IOPS. When the credits run out — which happens after roughly 30 minutes of sustained 3,000 IOPS on a 100 GB volume — performance collapses to 300 IOPS. Applications do not receive warnings. CloudWatch metrics will show BurstBalance declining, but unless you have alarms configured, you will discover the problem when your query times triple at 2 AM.

The burst credit math:

Credit balance: starts at 5,400,000 (enough for 30 minutes at 3,000 IOPS)
Credit earn rate: baseline_IOPS * 1 credit/second
Credit consumption: (actual_IOPS - baseline_IOPS) * 1 credit/second

For a 200 GB gp2 volume (600 IOPS baseline):
- Burst IOPS available: 3,000
- Consumption rate at full burst: 2,400 credits/second
- Depletion time: 5,400,000 / 2,400 = 37.5 minutes

For volumes over 1 TB (baseline > 3,000 IOPS), the burst mechanism no longer applies because the baseline already meets or exceeds 3,000 IOPS. This is why many teams accidentally run fine on gp2 for years — their storage grew large enough to push past the burst ceiling before they noticed.

#### gp3: The Right Default for Most Production Workloads

gp3 decouples IOPS from storage size entirely. Every gp3 volume starts with 3,000 IOPS and 125 MB/s throughput included in the base price, regardless of volume size. You can provision up to 16,000 IOPS and 1,000 MB/s independently, paying only for what you configure above the baseline.

This matters practically: a 100 GB gp3 volume gets the same guaranteed 3,000 IOPS as a 10 TB gp3 volume at base pricing. No burst credits, no performance collapse, no minimum storage floor to hit an IOPS target.

Pricing structure (verify exact current rates for your region in the AWS Pricing Calculator before budgeting — EBS pricing has changed over time and varies by region):

| Volume Type | Storage Cost | IOPS Cost | Throughput Cost |

|---|---|---|---|

| gp2 | Per GB-month, IOPS bundled into the rate | Included (tied to size) | Included |

| gp3 | Per GB-month, lower than gp2's rate | 3,000 IOPS included free, then billed per additional provisioned IOPS | 125 MB/s included free, then billed per additional MB/s |

| io1 | Per GB-month, higher than gp2/gp3 | Billed per provisioned IOPS | Included |

| io2 | Same per-GB rate as io1 | Billed per provisioned IOPS (tiered — lower per-unit rate at very high IOPS) | Included |

The storage cost alone makes gp3 cheaper per GB than gp2. The bigger driver of savings, though, is structural: on a volume that needs IOPS well above the 3-IOPS-per-GB gp2 ratio, gp2 forces you to over-provision storage just to reach the IOPS target, while gp3 lets you buy the IOPS and the storage independently. Run the actual numbers for your target IOPS and storage size against current pricing before committing — the relative savings depend on how far your IOPS requirement outpaces what gp2's ratio would force you to provision, not on a fixed percentage.

io1 vs io2: When You Need Guaranteed IOPS

Provisioned IOPS volumes exist for workloads where gp3's 16,000 IOPS ceiling is insufficient, or where you need single-digit millisecond latency guarantees that you are willing to pay for explicitly.

#### io1: The Workhorse Provisioned Tier

io1 allows up to 64,000 IOPS per volume (on Nitro-based instances) and up to 1,000 MB/s throughput. The IOPS-to-storage ratio constraint is critical: you can provision a maximum of 50 IOPS per GB of storage. A volume needing 64,000 IOPS requires at minimum 1,280 GB of storage, even if your actual data fits in 200 GB. This is the io1 "storage tax" — you pay for storage you do not need just to satisfy the ratio constraint.

io1 durability is 99.8–99.9% (an annual failure rate of between 0.1% and 0.2% per volume). This sounds low, but io2 improves it dramatically.

#### io2: io1's Successor with Better Economics

io2 offers 99.999% durability (100x more durable than io1), the same IOPS ceiling of 64,000 per volume, and a more favorable IOPS-to-storage ratio of 500 IOPS per GB (10x better than io1). The 500:1 ratio means that same 64,000 IOPS volume only requires 128 GB of storage rather than 1,280 GB, eliminating most of the storage tax.

Per-IOPS pricing between io1 and io2 is the same, making io2 strictly better than io1 for new provisioning — you get the same IOPS cost with a far better IOPS-to-storage ratio and materially higher durability. AWS has not officially deprecated io1, but the industry direction is clear: io2 is the recommended provisioned tier.

io2 Block Express (available on R5b, X2idn, X2iedn, and newer instance families) extends the ceiling further: 256,000 IOPS, 4,000 MB/s throughput, and sub-millisecond P99 latency. For extreme OLTP workloads — financial transaction systems, high-frequency trading audit logs, real-time fraud detection — this is the tier to evaluate.

When provisioned IOPS actually makes sense:

  • → You need more than 16,000 IOPS (gp3 ceiling)
  • → Your workload has consistent I/O patterns and latency sensitivity that gp3's shared infrastructure cannot guarantee
  • → You have a mixed read/write ratio with concurrent I/O from dozens of connections
  • → Regulatory requirements mandate documented performance guarantees (some compliance frameworks require it)

    • When provisioned IOPS is overkill:

  • → Workloads with high average IOPS but acceptable burst latency
  • → Development and staging environments
  • → Read-heavy workloads with caching layers (ElastiCache, readable AG secondaries) absorbing the majority of reads
  • → Any workload under 16,000 sustained IOPS

    #### Terraform Configuration for Storage Tiers

    # terraform/modules/rds/main.tf
# Production-grade RDS module with parameterized storage configuration

variable "storage_config" {
  description = "Storage configuration for the RDS instance"
  type = object({
    type              = string
    allocated_gb      = number
    iops              = optional(number)
    throughput_mbps   = optional(number)
    max_allocated_gb  = optional(number)  # enables autoscaling
  })

  validation {
    condition = contains(["gp2", "gp3", "io1", "io2"], var.storage_config.type)
    error_message = "storage_config.type must be one of: gp2, gp3, io1, io2"
  }

  validation {
    condition = !(var.storage_config.type == "gp2" && var.storage_config.iops != null)
    error_message = "gp2 does not support explicit IOPS configuration"
  }

  validation {
    condition = !(
      contains(["io1", "io2"], var.storage_config.type) &&
      var.storage_config.iops == null
    )
    error_message = "io1 and io2 require explicit IOPS configuration"
  }
}

locals {
  # io1 max ratio: 50 IOPS/GB; io2 max ratio: 500 IOPS/GB
  max_iops_ratio = {
    io1 = 50
    io2 = 500
    gp3 = 500  # gp3 allows up to 500 IOPS/GB above 3000 baseline
    gp2 = 3    # implicit, not configurable
  }

  # Validate io1/io2 IOPS-to-storage ratio
  iops_to_storage_ratio = (
    var.storage_config.iops != null ?
    var.storage_config.iops / var.storage_config.allocated_gb :
    0
  )
}

resource "aws_db_instance" "this" {
  identifier     = var.db_identifier
  engine         = var.engine
  engine_version = var.engine_version
  instance_class = var.instance_class

  # Storage configuration
  allocated_storage     = var.storage_config.allocated_gb
  max_allocated_storage = var.storage_config.max_allocated_gb  # null disables autoscaling
  storage_type          = var.storage_config.type
  iops                  = var.storage_config.iops
  storage_throughput    = var.storage_config.throughput_mbps  # gp3 only

  storage_encrypted = true
  kms_key_id        = var.kms_key_arn

  # Prevent accidental deletion
  deletion_protection       = var.environment == "prod"
  skip_final_snapshot       = var.environment != "prod"
  final_snapshot_identifier = var.environment == "prod" ? "${var.db_identifier}-final" : null

  tags = merge(var.common_tags, {
    StorageTier     = var.storage_config.type
    ProvisionedIOPS = tostring(coalesce(var.storage_config.iops, 0))
  })
}

# Outputs for downstream monitoring configuration
output "storage_details" {
  value = {
    type         = aws_db_instance.this.storage_type
    allocated_gb = aws_db_instance.this.allocated_storage
    iops         = aws_db_instance.this.iops
    throughput   = aws_db_instance.this.storage_throughput
    arn          = aws_db_instance.this.arn
  }
}

    Storage Autoscaling, Modification Limits, and the Gotchas Nobody Tells You

    #### Storage Can Only Grow

    This is the most important operational constraint in RDS storage management: you cannot shrink allocated storage. Once you provision 2 TB, you are paying for 2 TB forever on that instance. The only escape is a snapshot restore to a new instance with smaller storage, which requires downtime or a blue-green deployment.

    The practical consequence: start conservatively and use autoscaling.

    # Enable storage autoscaling on an existing instance
# max-allocated-storage must be at least 10% larger than allocated-storage
aws rds modify-db-instance \
  --db-instance-identifier prod-sqlserver-01 \
  --max-allocated-storage 2000 \
  --apply-immediately

# Disable autoscaling by setting max equal to current allocated (not below)
aws rds modify-db-instance \
  --db-instance-identifier prod-sqlserver-01 \
  --max-allocated-storage 500 \
  --apply-immediately

    RDS autoscaling triggers when: free storage falls below 10% of allocated storage AND remains there for 5 minutes, AND at least 6 hours have passed since the last autoscale event. It increases storage in increments of either 10 GB or 10% of current allocation, whichever is larger. It will not scale above max-allocated-storage.

    The 6-hour cooldown is a real operational risk. If your database has a runaway load that consumes storage at 1 GB/minute, autoscaling at the 5-minute threshold fires once, adds 50 GB, and then is locked for 6 hours while storage continues filling. The right pattern is autoscaling plus a CloudWatch alarm on free storage at the 15% threshold so you have warning before autoscaling even activates.

    #### The Modification Window Problem

    Storage type changes (e.g., gp2 to gp3) use --apply-immediately, but storage size increases do not always complete immediately. A storage modification on a running instance triggers an optimization phase that AWS calls "storage optimization" — this is essentially an online resize. Depending on volume size, this can take minutes to hours. During optimization, the instance remains available, but:

  • → You cannot trigger another storage modification
  • → The instance shows status `storage-optimization` in `describe-db-instances`
  • → Failover times for Multi-AZ may increase slightly during optimization
    • # Check if a storage modification is still in progress before attempting another
aws rds describe-db-instances \

  --query 'DBInstances[0].{
    Status:DBInstanceStatus,
    PendingValues:PendingModifiedValues,
    StorageType:StorageType,
    AllocatedStorage:AllocatedStorage,
    IOPS:Iops
  }'

    #### Multi-AZ and Storage Modifications

    For Multi-AZ instances, storage modifications are applied to the standby first, then the primary. This means:

    1. AWS modifies the standby EBS volume

    2. A failover occurs (brief interruption, typically under 60 seconds)

    3. The former standby (now primary) runs on the new storage

    4. The former primary (now standby) gets its storage updated

    Applications connected with proper retry logic will reconnect automatically. Connection poolers like RDS Proxy absorb the failover without the application seeing it. If you are connecting directly to the RDS endpoint without retry logic, that 60-second window causes errors.

    *Multi-AZ storage changes go through a failover — apps without retry logic will see a brief connection error during step 3.*

    This also means --apply-immediately does not mean "instantly" for Multi-AZ instances — it means "during this maintenance event" rather than "at next maintenance window," but the actual modification still requires the failover cycle.

    Benchmarking Your Storage Tier Before Migrating Production

    Before migrating a workload from on-premises or from one storage tier to another, establish a baseline. This requires understanding what I/O profile your database actually has — sequential vs. random, read vs. write, queue depth.

    #!/usr/bin/env python3
"""
rds_storage_profiler.py
Profiles I/O patterns on an RDS instance using CloudWatch metrics to recommend
the appropriate storage tier.

Requires: boto3, configured AWS credentials with cloudwatch:GetMetricStatistics
and rds:DescribeDBInstances permissions.
"""

import boto3
import json
import sys
from datetime import datetime, timedelta, timezone
from dataclasses import dataclass, asdict
from typing import Optional

@dataclass
class StorageProfile:
    db_identifier: str
    storage_type: str
    allocated_gb: int
    provisioned_iops: Optional[int]
    avg_read_iops: float
    avg_write_iops: float
    peak_read_iops: float
    peak_write_iops: float
    avg_read_latency_ms: float
    avg_write_latency_ms: float
    avg_queue_depth: float
    avg_throughput_mbps: float
    burst_balance_min: Optional[float]
    recommended_tier: str
    recommendation_reason: str


def get_metric_stats(cw_client, db_id: str, metric: str, stat: str,
                     start: datetime, end: datetime, period: int = 300) -> list:
    response = cw_client.get_metric_statistics(
        Namespace='AWS/RDS',
        MetricName=metric,
        Dimensions=[{'Name': 'DBInstanceIdentifier', 'Value': db_id}],
        StartTime=start,
        EndTime=end,
        Period=period,
        Statistics=[stat]
    )
    return sorted(response['Datapoints'], key=lambda x: x['Timestamp'])


def profile_instance(db_identifier: str, lookback_days: int = 14) -> StorageProfile:
    rds = boto3.client('rds')
    cw = boto3.client('cloudwatch')

    end = datetime.now(timezone.utc)
    start = end - timedelta(days=lookback_days)

    # Get instance metadata
    desc = rds.describe_db_instances(DBInstanceIdentifier=db_identifier)
    inst = desc['DBInstances'][0]
    storage_type = inst['StorageType']
    allocated_gb = inst['AllocatedStorage']
    provisioned_iops = inst.get('Iops')

    def avg_metric(metric, stat='Average'):
        points = get_metric_stats(cw, db_identifier, metric, stat, start, end)
        if not points:
            return 0.0
        return sum(p[stat] for p in points) / len(points)

    def max_metric(metric):
        points = get_metric_stats(cw, db_identifier, metric, 'Maximum', start, end)
        if not points:
            return 0.0
        return max(p['Maximum'] for p in points)

    avg_read_iops  = avg_metric('ReadIOPS')
    avg_write_iops = avg_metric('WriteIOPS')
    peak_read_iops  = max_metric('ReadIOPS')
    peak_write_iops = max_metric('WriteIOPS')

    # Latency in seconds from CloudWatch, convert to ms
    avg_read_latency_ms  = avg_metric('ReadLatency') * 1000
    avg_write_latency_ms = avg_metric('WriteLatency') * 1000

    avg_queue_depth = avg_metric('DiskQueueDepth')

    # Throughput: ReadThroughput + WriteThroughput (bytes/sec → MB/s)
    avg_throughput_mbps = (
        avg_metric('ReadThroughput') + avg_metric('WriteThroughput')
    ) / (1024 * 1024)

    # BurstBalance only relevant for gp2
    burst_balance_min = None
    if storage_type == 'gp2':
        points = get_metric_stats(cw, db_identifier, 'BurstBalance', 'Minimum', start, end)
        if points:
            burst_balance_min = min(p['Minimum'] for p in points)

    peak_total_iops = peak_read_iops + peak_write_iops
    avg_total_iops  = avg_read_iops + avg_write_iops

    # Recommendation logic
    recommended_tier = 'gp3'
    recommendation_reason = ''

    if peak_total_iops > 64000:
        recommended_tier = 'io2'
        recommendation_reason = (
            f'Peak IOPS ({peak_total_iops:.0f}) exceeds gp3 ceiling of 16,000 '
            f'and io2/io1 ceiling of 64,000 — evaluate io2 Block Express on '
            f'supported instance families.'
        )
    elif peak_total_iops > 16000:
        recommended_tier = 'io2'
        recommendation_reason = (
            f'Peak IOPS ({peak_total_iops:.0f}) exceeds gp3 ceiling of 16,000. '
            f'io2 recommended over io1 for identical cost and higher durability.'
        )
    elif avg_write_latency_ms > 5.0 or avg_read_latency_ms > 5.0:
        recommended_tier = 'io2'
        recommendation_reason = (
            f'Latency exceeds 5ms average (read: {avg_read_latency_ms:.2f}ms, '
            f'write: {avg_write_latency_ms:.2f}ms). Provisioned IOPS provides '
            f'more consistent sub-millisecond latency guarantees.'
        )
    elif avg_queue_depth > 10:
        recommended_tier = 'io2'
        recommendation_reason = (
            f'High average queue depth ({avg_queue_depth:.1f}) indicates I/O '
            f'saturation. Provisioned IOPS will reduce queuing latency.'
        )
    elif storage_type == 'gp2':
        if burst_balance_min is not None and burst_balance_min < 50:
            recommended_tier = 'gp3'
            recommendation_reason = (
                f'Currently on gp2 with minimum burst balance of '
                f'{burst_balance_min:.1f}%. Performance is credit-constrained. '
                f'Migrating to gp3 eliminates burst risk at lower cost per GB.'
            )
        else:
            recommended_tier = 'gp3'
            recommendation_reason = (
                'Currently on gp2. gp3 is cheaper per GB and provides '
                'guaranteed 3,000 IOPS baseline without burst credits. '
                'Migrate to gp3 for equivalent or better performance at '
                'reduced cost.'
            )
    elif peak_total_iops < 8000 and avg_total_iops < 3000:
        recommended_tier = 'gp3'
        recommendation_reason = (
            f'Workload is within gp3 baseline (avg: {avg_total_iops:.0f} IOPS, '
            f'peak: {peak_total_iops:.0f} IOPS). gp3 at base configuration is '
            f'sufficient and most cost-effective.'
        )
    else:
        recommended_tier = 'gp3'
        recommendation_reason = (
            f'Peak IOPS ({peak_total_iops:.0f}) within gp3 range. Configure '
            f'gp3 with {int(peak_total_iops * 1.2):.0f} IOPS to provide '
            f'20% headroom above observed peak.'
        )

    return StorageProfile(
        db_identifier=db_identifier,
        storage_type=storage_type,
        allocated_gb=allocated_gb,
        provisioned_iops=provisioned_iops,
        avg_read_iops=round(avg_read_iops, 1),
        avg_write_iops=round(avg_write_iops, 1),
        peak_read_iops=round(peak_read_iops, 1),
        peak_write_iops=round(peak_write_iops, 1),
        avg_read_latency_ms=round(avg_read_latency_ms, 3),
        avg_write_latency_ms=round(avg_write_latency_ms, 3),
        avg_queue_depth=round(avg_queue_depth, 2),
        avg_throughput_mbps=round(avg_throughput_mbps, 2),
        burst_balance_min=round(burst_balance_min, 1) if burst_balance_min is not None else None,
        recommended_tier=recommended_tier,
        recommendation_reason=recommendation_reason,
    )


if __name__ == '__main__':
    if len(sys.argv) < 2:
        print("Usage: rds_storage_profiler.py <db-instance-identifier> [lookback-days]")
        sys.exit(1)

    db_id = sys.argv[1]
    lookback = int(sys.argv[2]) if len(sys.argv) > 2 else 14

    print(f"Profiling {db_id} over the last {lookback} days...")
    profile = profile_instance(db_id, lookback)
    print(json.dumps(asdict(profile), indent=2))

    Run this profiler against your existing RDS instances before making storage tier decisions. The 14-day lookback captures weekly patterns including business-day peaks and any scheduled batch jobs. For databases with monthly cycles (end-of-month reporting, billing runs), extend to 35 days.

    Workload-Specific Recommendations

    #### OLTP (Online Transaction Processing)

    OLTP workloads are characterized by many small, random I/Os with low latency requirements and a high write percentage. Typical patterns:

  • → Queue depth: 1–20
  • → Average I/O size: 4–16 KB
  • → Read/write ratio: 60/40 to 40/60
  • → Latency sensitivity: high (users feel > 10ms query times)

    • For OLTP under 16,000 IOPS: gp3 with IOPS configured to 120% of your measured peak. Set throughput to 500 MB/s unless you can measure a specific throughput requirement. Use autoscaling with a max 2–3x your starting allocation.

    For OLTP between 16,000 and 64,000 IOPS: io2. The IOPS guarantee matters here — a gp3 volume sharing underlying infrastructure with other tenants can experience latency variability during neighbor-noisy periods. io2's dedicated provisioning removes that variable.

    SQL Server-specific: Monitor WRITELOG and PAGEIOLATCH_* waits to understand write pressure. Excessive log-write waits can appear as a storage problem when log throughput tuning (right-sized log file, faster log volume) would solve it more cheaply than a tier upgrade.

    -- SQL Server: check write/log pressure vs buffer pressure
-- Run this to evaluate whether storage pressure is
-- workload-driven or configuration-driven

SELECT wait_type,
       waiting_tasks_count,
       wait_time_ms / 1000.0 AS wait_seconds,
       100.0 * wait_time_ms / SUM(wait_time_ms) OVER() AS pct_of_waits
FROM sys.dm_os_wait_stats
WHERE wait_type IN ('WRITELOG', 'PAGEIOLATCH_SH', 'PAGEIOLATCH_EX',
                    'PAGEIOLATCH_UP', 'LOGBUFFER', 'LOGMGR_RESERVE_APPEND')
ORDER BY wait_time_ms DESC;

-- High WRITELOG relative to PAGEIOLATCH: the log volume is the
-- bottleneck -- move it to faster storage before upgrading the tier.
-- High PAGEIOLATCH_SH with low Page Life Expectancy: the buffer pool
-- is undersized or the workload is genuinely I/O-bound.
    -- SQL Server: check I/O stall statistics to identify storage pressure
-- vs. locking or other wait types
-- Run on RDS SQL Server via SSMS or sqlcmd

SELECT
    DB_NAME(vfs.database_id)          AS database_name,
    mf.physical_name,
    mf.type_desc,
    vfs.io_stall_read_ms,
    vfs.num_of_reads,
    CASE WHEN vfs.num_of_reads = 0 THEN 0
         ELSE vfs.io_stall_read_ms / vfs.num_of_reads
    END                               AS avg_read_stall_ms,
    vfs.io_stall_write_ms,
    vfs.num_of_writes,
    CASE WHEN vfs.num_of_writes = 0 THEN 0
         ELSE vfs.io_stall_write_ms / vfs.num_of_writes
    END                               AS avg_write_stall_ms,
    vfs.io_stall,
    vfs.num_of_bytes_read / 1048576.0 AS mb_read,
    vfs.num_of_bytes_written / 1048576.0 AS mb_written
FROM sys.dm_io_virtual_file_stats(NULL, NULL) vfs
JOIN sys.master_files mf
    ON vfs.database_id = mf.database_id
    AND vfs.file_id = mf.file_id
WHERE DB_NAME(vfs.database_id) NOT IN ('master', 'model', 'msdb', 'tempdb')
ORDER BY vfs.io_stall DESC;

-- Decision rule:
-- avg_read_stall_ms or avg_write_stall_ms consistently > 20ms:
--   Storage tier upgrade likely needed
-- avg stall < 5ms but high IOPS: you're at gp3 ceiling, evaluate io2
-- avg stall > 20ms but low IOPS: check for lock contention,
--   inefficient queries before blaming storage

    #### Analytics and Data Warehousing

    Analytics workloads are the inverse of OLTP: large sequential scans, high throughput requirements, lower IOPS sensitivity per query (but many concurrent queries compound this), and predominantly read-heavy with periodic bulk loads.

    For Redshift, BigQuery, or Athena you are not picking EBS storage types directly — those services manage their own storage. But for SQL Server used for mixed OLTP/reporting:

  • → Throughput matters more than IOPS. A 500 MB/s sequential read is one IOPS at 500 MB transfer size, or 125,000 IOPS at 4 KB. CloudWatch `ReadThroughput` is more meaningful than `ReadIOPS` for these workloads.
  • → gp3 at maximum throughput (1,000 MB/s) is often sufficient. Analytics queries scanning hundreds of GBs benefit from high sequential throughput that gp3 can provide without the cost of provisioned IOPS.
  • → Readable secondaries change the calculus. If reporting queries run against a readable AG secondary rather than the primary, the primary's storage only needs to handle write I/O. The secondary can be on a different storage tier than the primary.
    • # Reporting offload for SQL Server: RDS has no read replicas, so route
# reporting reads to a readable secondary. On Azure, provision a Hyperscale
# named replica on its own storage configuration; on self-managed EC2/VMs,
# add an Always On AG readable secondary whose disks are sized for the
# reporting workload independently of the primary.
az sql db replica create \
  --name prod-sqlserver-reporting \
  --server prod-sql-server \
  --resource-group prod-rg \
  --secondary-type Named \
  --compute-model Provisioned \
  --service-objective HS_Gen5_4

# Note: the readable secondary's storage and compute are sized for the
# reporting workload — the primary's storage only needs to handle write I/O.

    #### Mixed Workloads: OLTP + Reporting on the Same Instance

    The most common real-world RDS deployment is not pure OLTP or pure analytics — it is a production OLTP database that also serves some reporting queries, either during business hours or in scheduled overnight batch windows. This creates competing I/O patterns:

  • → Daytime: random 4–16 KB reads/writes, low queue depth, latency-sensitive
  • → Overnight: large sequential scans for reporting, high throughput demand

    • Storage tier selection for mixed workloads should optimize for the latency-sensitive daytime OLTP pattern, because:

    1. Report queries can be made to wait; user-facing OLTP cannot

    2. Query plans for reports can often be rewritten to use indexes, reducing scan I/O

    3. Read replicas or scheduled reports against S3 exports are architectural alternatives

    For a mixed workload, configure gp3 at the IOPS level needed for daytime OLTP peaks, with throughput at 500–1,000 MB/s. If overnight reports are degrading the primary, the right fix is a read replica or a scheduled export to S3 followed by Athena queries — not a storage tier upgrade.

    Decision Framework: Picking the Right Tier

    Use this decision tree before provisioning any RDS storage:

    START
│
├─ Is this dev/test/staging?
│   └─ YES → gp3, base configuration (no IOPS override needed)
│              Rationale: 3,000 IOPS baseline sufficient for non-production
│
├─ Do you need > 64,000 sustained IOPS?
│   └─ YES → io2 Block Express on supported instance types
│              If not available, consider sharding or readable secondaries
│
├─ Do you need > 16,000 sustained IOPS?
│   └─ YES → io2 (identical price to io1, better durability, better ratio)
│              Size storage to satisfy 500 IOPS/GB constraint
│
├─ Is your workload latency-sensitive AND did benchmarking show
│   >5ms average latency on gp3?
│   └─ YES → Evaluate io2. But first: check whether the latency comes
│              from storage vs. lock contention (see SQL Server/PG queries above)
│
├─ Is your workload currently on gp2?
│   └─ YES → Migrate to gp3. Always. No exceptions.
│              Even if gp2 is "working," gp3 is cheaper and more predictable.
│
└─ Everything else → gp3
    Configure IOPS to 120% of measured peak.
    Configure throughput to 500 MB/s unless measurement shows you need more.
    Enable autoscaling with 2x headroom on max_allocated_storage.

    The "migrate gp2 to gp3" branch deserves emphasis. There is no scenario where gp2 is preferable to gp3 for a new provisioning decision. The only reason gp2 instances exist in 2024 is organizational inertia — they were created before gp3 was available (gp3 launched in late 2020) or before teams updated their provisioning playbooks.

    Monitoring Storage Health in Production

    Beyond the burst balance alarm covered earlier, a complete storage monitoring setup for RDS should include:

    #!/usr/bin/env python3
"""
rds_storage_monitor.py
Creates a comprehensive CloudWatch dashboard and alarm set for RDS storage health.
Covers gp2 burst, IOPS saturation, latency thresholds, and free storage.
"""

import boto3
import json
from typing import Any

def create_storage_alarms(
    db_identifier: str,
    storage_type: str,
    provisioned_iops: int | None,
    sns_topic_arn: str,
    region: str = 'us-east-1'
) -> list[str]:
    """
    Creates appropriate CloudWatch alarms based on storage tier.
    Returns list of created alarm names.
    """
    cw = boto3.client('cloudwatch', region_name=region)
    created_alarms = []

    common_dims = [{'Name': 'DBInstanceIdentifier', 'Value': db_identifier}]

    def put_alarm(name: str, metric: str, stat: str, threshold: float,
                  operator: str, period: int, eval_periods: int,
                  description: str, unit: str | None = None) -> None:
        kwargs: dict[str, Any] = dict(
            AlarmName=f'RDS-{db_identifier}-{name}',
            AlarmDescription=description,
            MetricName=metric,
            Namespace='AWS/RDS',
            Statistic=stat,
            Dimensions=common_dims,
            Period=period,
            EvaluationPeriods=eval_periods,
            Threshold=threshold,
            ComparisonOperator=operator,
            AlarmActions=[sns_topic_arn],
            OKActions=[sns_topic_arn],
            TreatMissingData='missing',
        )
        if unit:
            kwargs['Unit'] = unit
        cw.put_metric_alarm(**kwargs)
        created_alarms.append(kwargs['AlarmName'])

    # Free storage alarm — 15% threshold gives time to act before autoscaling
    # Uses absolute bytes: 15% of typical starting sizes
    # For production, calculate 15% of allocated storage in bytes
    put_alarm(
        name='FreeStorageLow',
        metric='FreeStorageSpace',
        stat='Average',
        threshold=10 * 1024 ** 3,  # 10 GB floor as a starting point
        operator='LessThanThreshold',
        period=300,
        eval_periods=3,
        description=(
            f'Free storage on {db_identifier} below 10 GB. '
            'Review growth rate and autoscaling configuration.'
        ),
        unit='Bytes',
    )

    # Write latency — threshold tuned per tier
    write_latency_threshold = 0.005  # 5ms for gp3
    if storage_type in ('io1', 'io2'):
        write_latency_threshold = 0.002  # 2ms for provisioned

    put_alarm(
        name='WriteLatencyHigh',
        metric='WriteLatency',
        stat='Average',
        threshold=write_latency_threshold,
        operator='GreaterThanThreshold',
        period=60,
        eval_periods=5,
        description=(
            f'Write latency on {db_identifier} exceeded '
            f'{write_latency_threshold * 1000:.0f}ms average for 5 minutes.'
        ),
        unit='Seconds',
    )

    put_alarm(
        name='ReadLatencyHigh',
        metric='ReadLatency',
        stat='Average',
        threshold=write_latency_threshold,
        operator='GreaterThanThreshold',
        period=60,
        eval_periods=5,
        description=(
            f'Read latency on {db_identifier} exceeded '
            f'{write_latency_threshold * 1000:.0f}ms average for 5 minutes.'
        ),
        unit='Seconds',
    )

    # Queue depth — sustained > 64 indicates serious I/O saturation
    put_alarm(
        name='DiskQueueDepthHigh',
        metric='DiskQueueDepth',
        stat='Average',
        threshold=64,
        operator='GreaterThanThreshold',
        period=300,
        eval_periods=3,
        description=(
            f'Disk queue depth on {db_identifier} sustained above 64. '
            'I/O is saturated — consider storage tier upgrade or query optimization.'
        ),
    )

    # IOPS saturation — 90% of provisioned or gp3 maximum
    if provisioned_iops:
        iops_ceiling = provisioned_iops
    elif storage_type == 'gp3':
        iops_ceiling = 16000
    else:  # gp2
        iops_ceiling = 16000  # theoretical max; burst balance alarm is more useful

    put_alarm(
        name='IOPSNearCeiling',
        metric='ReadIOPS',
        stat='Average',
        threshold=iops_ceiling * 0.9,
        operator='GreaterThanThreshold',
        period=300,
        eval_periods=3,
        description=(
            f'Read IOPS on {db_identifier} at 90% of '
            f'provisioned ceiling ({iops_ceiling}). '
            'Review workload or increase IOPS provisioning.'
        ),
    )

    # gp2-specific: burst balance
    if storage_type == 'gp2':
        put_alarm(
            name='BurstBalanceLow',
            metric='BurstBalance',
            stat='Minimum',
            threshold=30,
            operator='LessThanThreshold',
            period=300,
            eval_periods=2,
            description=(
                f'gp2 burst balance on {db_identifier} below 30%. '
                'Performance degradation likely. Migrate to gp3 immediately.'
            ),
        )

    return created_alarms


if __name__ == '__main__':
    import sys

    if len(sys.argv) < 4:
        print("Usage: rds_storage_monitor.py <db-id> <storage-type> "
              "<sns-topic-arn> [provisioned-iops]")
        sys.exit(1)

    db_id         = sys.argv[1]
    stype         = sys.argv[2]
    sns_arn       = sys.argv[3]
    prov_iops     = int(sys.argv[4]) if len(sys.argv) > 4 else None

    alarms = create_storage_alarms(db_id, stype, prov_iops, sns_arn)
    print(f"Created {len(alarms)} alarms:")
    for a in alarms:
        print(f"  {a}")

    Migration Playbook: gp2 to gp3 at Scale

    For environments with many RDS instances, a systematic gp2-to-gp3 migration requires inventory, planning, and staged execution. Here is the operational procedure:

    #!/bin/bash
# gp2_to_gp3_migration.sh
# Identifies all gp2 RDS instances in an account/region and
# generates a migration plan with cost comparison.
# Execute the actual migrations separately after review.

set -euo pipefail

REGION="${AWS_DEFAULT_REGION:-us-east-1}"
OUTPUT_DIR="./gp2_migration_$(date +%Y%m%d_%H%M%S)"
mkdir -p "$OUTPUT_DIR"

echo "Scanning for gp2 RDS instances in $REGION..."

# Get all gp2 instances
aws rds describe-db-instances \
  --region "$REGION" \
  --query 'DBInstances[?StorageType==`gp2`].{
    ID:DBInstanceIdentifier,
    Class:DBInstanceClass,
    Engine:Engine,
    Status:DBInstanceStatus,
    StorageGB:AllocatedStorage,
    MultiAZ:MultiAZ,
    Tags:TagList
  }' \
  --output json > "$OUTPUT_DIR/gp2_instances.json"

INSTANCE_COUNT=$(jq length "$OUTPUT_DIR/gp2_instances.json")
echo "Found $INSTANCE_COUNT gp2 instances."

# Generate migration commands with cost comparison
jq -r '.[] | .ID + " " + (.StorageGB | tostring)' \
    "$OUTPUT_DIR/gp2_instances.json" | \
while IFS=' ' read -r DB_ID STORAGE_GB; do
    # gp2 IOPS (3 per GB, min 100, max 16000)
    GP2_IOPS=$(( STORAGE_GB * 3 ))
    if (( GP2_IOPS < 100 )); then GP2_IOPS=100; fi
    if (( GP2_IOPS > 16000 )); then GP2_IOPS=16000; fi

    # Target gp3 IOPS: max(3000, gp2_equivalent)
    # Add 20% headroom
    TARGET_IOPS=$(( GP2_IOPS > 3000 ? GP2_IOPS * 12 / 10 : 3000 ))
    if (( TARGET_IOPS > 16000 )); then TARGET_IOPS=16000; fi

    cat >> "$OUTPUT_DIR/migration_commands.sh" <<EOF
# Migrate $DB_ID: ${STORAGE_GB}GB gp2 → gp3 (${TARGET_IOPS} IOPS)
aws rds modify-db-instance \\
  --db-instance-identifier "$DB_ID" \\
  --storage-type gp3 \\
  --iops $TARGET_IOPS \\
  --storage-throughput 500 \\
  --region "$REGION"
  # Remove --apply-immediately for maintenance window scheduling
  # Add --apply-immediately to execute within minutes

EOF
done

echo "Migration plan written to $OUTPUT_DIR/"
echo "Review $OUTPUT_DIR/migration_commands.sh before executing."
echo ""
echo "IMPORTANT CHECKLIST before running migrations:"
echo "  1. Verify BurstBalance CloudWatch alarms exist for each gp2 instance"
echo "  2. Confirm Multi-AZ instances have retry logic or RDS Proxy in place"
echo "  3. Schedule migrations during low-traffic windows for gp2 volumes > 1TB"
echo "  4. Monitor storage-optimization status after each modification"
echo "  5. Validate performance metrics 24-48 hours post-migration"

    The migration from gp2 to gp3 is online — no maintenance window required, no downtime for the database engine. The underlying EBS optimization process runs while the instance serves traffic. For Multi-AZ, the failover cycle described earlier applies, but modern applications with connection retry should handle it transparently.

    Beyond AWS: Azure and GCP Storage for SQL Server

    The gp2/gp3/io1/io2 decision is AWS-specific; the underlying question — how much IOPS and throughput does the SQL Server workload need, at what latency, at what price — is universal. On Azure: Premium SSD v2 and Ultra Disk are the SQL Server workhorses (Ultra Disk for sub-millisecond log latency on demanding workloads), with the caching policies from Chapter 13 (data ReadOnly, log None) as important as the disk type. On GCP: Persistent Disk SSD (balanced vs performance tiers) with throughput scaling by disk size. The cross-cloud principles from this chapter transfer directly: separate data and log onto appropriate tiers, provision IOPS/throughput for the measured workload (not the hoped-for one), monitor latency (not just IOPS) as the primary signal, and re-evaluate when the workload changes. Storage is the one cloud component where the physics — latency, IOPS, throughput — is identical everywhere; only the product names change.

    Key Takeaways

  • → gp3 is the right default for virtually every new RDS provisioning decision. It decouples IOPS from storage size, costs less per GB than gp2, and provides a guaranteed 3,000 IOPS baseline with no burst credit mechanics. The only reasons to choose otherwise are specific IOPS or latency requirements that gp3 cannot satisfy.
  • → gp2's burst credit system is a silent performance trap. Volumes under 1 TB will eventually deplete burst credits under sustained load, dropping to as few as 300 IOPS with no application-level warning. If you have gp2 instances in production, instrument `BurstBalance` in CloudWatch and migrate to gp3 before the next burst event.
  • → io2 is strictly superior to io1 for provisioned IOPS workloads. Same price, 100x higher durability rating, and a 500 IOPS/GB ratio versus io1's 50 IOPS/GB — meaning less "storage tax" to meet ratio constraints. Use io2 whenever your workload genuinely exceeds gp3's 16,000 IOPS ceiling or requires single-digit millisecond latency guarantees under sustained concurrent load.
  • → RDS storage can only grow, never shrink. Plan initial allocations conservatively and use `max_allocated_storage` autoscaling for organic growth. Reducing storage requires a snapshot restore to a new instance, which demands downtime or a blue-green deployment. The 6-hour autoscaling cooldown is a real operational risk for rapidly-growing databases — pair autoscaling with a 15% free-storage CloudWatch alarm.
  • → Storage tier decisions should follow measured workload data, not estimates. Use CloudWatch metrics — `ReadIOPS`, `WriteIOPS`, `ReadLatency`, `WriteLatency`, `DiskQueueDepth` — over a 14-day minimum lookback to understand actual peak and average I/O patterns before provisioning. Upgrading from gp3 to io2 without measurement often wastes money; staying on gp2 or under-provisioning gp3 without measurement silently degrades production performance. The `rds_storage_profiler.py` script in this chapter automates that analysis and produces a tier recommendation you can act on directly.
