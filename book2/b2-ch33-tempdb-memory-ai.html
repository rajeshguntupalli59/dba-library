Chapter 33: AI Diagnosis of TempDB and Memory Pressure

TempDB and memory pressure are the classic ambiguous emergencies: slow queries, timeouts, and a wait-stat profile that points everywhere and nowhere at once. Is it version-store bloat from a long-running snapshot transaction? Sort and hash spills from a bad plan? Genuine memory starvation? Allocation contention on tempdb data files? A human DBA answers these questions by correlating half a dozen DMVs against workload knowledge built over years — which is exactly why this is fertile ground for AI. This chapter is about the AI layer on top of tempdb and memory diagnostics: ML models that classify wait signatures into root causes, anomaly detectors that learn your workload's normal memory rhythms, and LLM triage that reads a diagnostic snapshot and narrows the field from "something is wrong" to "here is the specific mechanism" in minutes instead of hours.

The base mechanics — how tempdb allocation works, what page life expectancy means — belong to the performance books. Here we assume you know the DMVs and focus on what changes when machine reasoning sits between the telemetry and the diagnosis.

---

Wait Signatures as Features, Not Just Symptoms

A DBA reads wait stats as symptoms and reasons backward to causes. A classifier does the same thing, except it learns the mapping from thousands of labeled examples instead of a career of incidents. The input is a wait vector — the delta of sys.dm_os_wait_stats over a fixed window, normalized — and the output is a root-cause class:

Memory grant pressure: RESOURCE_SEMAPHORE dominant, often with SOS_SCHEDULER_YIELD rising as queries queue.

Tempdb allocation contention: PAGELATCH_UP / PAGELATCH_EX concentrated on tempdb pages (2:1:1, 2:1:3), with high allocation rates but modest version-store growth.

Version-store pressure: IO_COMPLETION and PAGEIOLATCH_* climbing alongside version-store space growth, frequently paired with long-running transactions under read-committed snapshot isolation.

Spill-driven tempdb growth: SOS_SCHEDULER_YIELD plus internal-object allocation spikes correlated with specific query hashes — the signature of sort/hash spills from misestimated plans.

The feature engineering matters more than the algorithm. Capture wait deltas on a fixed cadence, normalize each wait type by total waits in the window (so the model learns shapes, not magnitudes that shift with load), and append a few scalar features: memory grants pending, PLE, tempdb allocation rate. A gradient-boosted classifier or even multinomial logistic regression on these features routinely hits 85%+ accuracy on labeled incident data — because the wait signatures of these failure modes are genuinely distinct once you stop looking at single counters in isolation.

-- Capture a normalized wait vector for the classifier (run on a 5-minute cadence,
-- store both snapshots, compute the delta in your collector)
SELECT
    wait_type,
    wait_time_ms,
    waiting_tasks_count,
    CAST(wait_time_ms AS FLOAT)
        / NULLIF(SUM(wait_time_ms) OVER (), 0) AS wait_share
FROM sys.dm_os_wait_stats
WHERE wait_type IN (
    'RESOURCE_SEMAPHORE', 'RESOURCE_SEMAPHORE_QUERY_COMPILE',
    'SOS_SCHEDULER_YIELD', 'PAGELATCH_UP', 'PAGELATCH_EX',
    'PAGEIOLATCH_SH', 'PAGEIOLATCH_EX', 'IO_COMPLETION',
    'CXPACKET', 'CXCONSUMER', 'LCK_M_X', 'LCK_M_S',
    'WRITELOG', 'LOGBUFFER', 'THREADPOOL'
)
ORDER BY wait_share DESC;

TempDB Signals the Model Needs

Wait vectors get you the class; tempdb-specific DMVs give you the mechanism and the culprit. Three queries form the model's tempdb context:

-- 1. What is consuming tempdb right now, by category
SELECT
    SUM(unallocated_extent_page_count) / 128.0 AS free_mb,
    SUM(version_store_reserved_page_count) / 128.0 AS version_store_mb,
    SUM(user_object_reserved_page_count) / 128.0 AS user_objects_mb,
    SUM(internal_object_reserved_page_count) / 128.0 AS internal_objects_mb,
    SUM(mixed_extent_page_count) / 128.0 AS mixed_extent_mb
FROM tempdb.sys.dm_db_file_space_usage;

-- 2. Who is allocating: top sessions by tempdb usage right now
SELECT TOP 10
    s.session_id,
    s.login_name,
    s.program_name,
    (t.user_objects_alloc_page_count - t.user_objects_dealloc_page_count) / 128.0
        AS user_objects_mb,
    (t.internal_objects_alloc_page_count - t.internal_objects_dealloc_page_count) / 128.0
        AS internal_objects_mb,
    DATEDIFF(SECOND, s.last_request_start_time, GETUTCDATE()) AS request_age_sec,
    LEFT(st.text, 200) AS query_shape
FROM sys.dm_db_task_space_usage t
JOIN sys.dm_exec_sessions s ON s.session_id = t.session_id
OUTER APPLY sys.dm_exec_sql_text(s.sql_handle) st
WHERE t.session_id > 50
ORDER BY (t.user_objects_alloc_page_count + t.internal_objects_alloc_page_count) DESC;

-- 3. Version store: is an old snapshot transaction pinning cleanup?
SELECT TOP 5
    t.transaction_id,
    t.transaction_sequence_num,
    DATEDIFF(SECOND, t.transaction_begin_time, GETUTCDATE()) AS age_sec,
    s.session_id,
    s.program_name
FROM sys.dm_tran_active_snapshot_database_transactions t
LEFT JOIN sys.dm_exec_sessions s ON s.session_id = t.session_id
ORDER BY t.transaction_begin_time;

The AI leverage here is twofold. First, allocation-rate anomaly detection: a model trained on per-session allocation rates learns the difference between your steady-state ETL tempdb usage and a runaway spill — something no static threshold captures, because your normal varies by hour and by day. Second, version-store aging analysis: the model correlates version-store growth with the oldest open snapshot transaction, so instead of "tempdb is growing," the diagnosis becomes "version store grew 18 GB in 40 minutes, pinned by transaction 98412 open for 52 minutes from the reporting job."

Memory Signals the Model Needs

For memory pressure, the model's context comes from sys.dm_os_performance_counters and sys.dm_exec_query_memory_grants:

-- Memory pressure snapshot for the diagnostic model
SELECT
    counter_name,
    cntr_value
FROM sys.dm_os_performance_counters
WHERE object_name LIKE '%Memory Manager%'
  AND counter_name IN (
      'Memory Grants Pending', 'Memory Grants Outstanding',
      'Target Server Memory (KB)', 'Total Server Memory (KB)',
      'Granted Workspace Memory (KB)', 'Maximum Workspace Memory (KB)'
  )
UNION ALL
SELECT
    'Page Life Expectancy' AS counter_name,
    cntr_value
FROM sys.dm_os_performance_counters
WHERE object_name LIKE '%Buffer Manager%'
  AND counter_name = 'Page life expectancy'
UNION ALL
SELECT
    'Lazy Writes/sec' AS counter_name,
    cntr_value
FROM sys.dm_os_performance_counters
WHERE object_name LIKE '%Buffer Manager%'
  AND counter_name = 'Lazy writes/sec';

-- Who is waiting for memory right now
SELECT TOP 10
    session_id,
    requested_memory_kb / 1024.0 AS requested_mb,
    granted_memory_kb / 1024.0 AS granted_mb,
    required_memory_kb / 1024.0 AS required_mb,
    wait_time_ms / 1000.0 AS wait_sec,
    wait_order,
    LEFT(st.text, 200) AS query_shape
FROM sys.dm_exec_query_memory_grants
OUTER APPLY sys.dm_exec_sql_text(sql_handle) st
WHERE grant_time IS NULL          -- still waiting, not yet granted
ORDER BY wait_time_ms DESC;

The key AI contribution on memory is learning your workload's normal rhythm. A static "page life expectancy below 300 seconds" rule pages you during every large batch load and stays silent during a genuine slow bleed at 3 AM. An anomaly model trained on PLE, lazy writes, and grants-pending histories learns that PLE craters every night at 1 AM during the ETL window (normal) but should never crater at 10 AM on a Tuesday (page someone). It also learns the slope signatures: a PLE cliff over 5 minutes means something very different from a PLE glide over 6 hours, and the model distinguishes them where a threshold cannot.

LLM Triage: A Worked Diagnosis

The classifier gives you a root-cause class; the LLM triage step turns the class plus the raw evidence into an actionable diagnosis with a named culprit and a next step. The orchestrator feeds the model the wait vector, the tempdb breakdown, the top allocators, the memory-grant waiters, and instance config (max server memory, tempdb file count, MAXDOP) — using the context-packing and structured-output patterns from Chapter 32.

Here is what distinguishes a good triage output from a generic one. Given version-store growth plus an old snapshot transaction, a weak model says "tempdb pressure detected, consider investigating long-running transactions." A well-prompted model says: "Version-store bloat: 18.4 GB version store (baseline 1.2 GB), pinned by transaction 98412 open 52 minutes from program 'NightlyReport'. Evidence: version_store_mb grew 16x in 40 min; oldest snapshot txn age 52 min; IO_COMPLETION waits up 9x. Recommended: ALERT_HUMAN with session kill candidate 214. Next diagnostic: check what NightlyReport session 214 is blocked on before killing."

The critical judgment being encoded: the model identifies the pinning transaction, not just the symptom, and it recommends checking blockers before killing — because killing a session that holds a 52-minute transaction triggers a long rollback that makes everything worse. That "check before you kill" reasoning is exactly the kind of senior-DBA judgment that belongs in the few-shot examples from Chapter 32.

import json
import pyodbc

TEMPDB_TRIAGE_PROMPT = """
You are a SQL Server tempdb and memory diagnostics expert. Classify the
pressure mechanism and identify the culprit session or transaction if any.

Mechanisms (choose exactly one):
- VERSION_STORE_BLOAT, ALLOCATION_CONTENTION, SPILL_PRESSURE,
  MEMORY_GRANT_STARVATION, BUFFER_POOL_PRESSURE, NO_PRESSURE

Respond as JSON:
{
  "mechanism": "<one of the above>",
  "culprit": "<session_id, transaction_id, or null>",
  "evidence": ["<observations citing input values>"],
  "confidence": <0.0-1.0>,
  "recommended_action": "ALERT_HUMAN|KILL_CANDIDATE|NO_ACTION",
  "kill_safety_note": "<rollback risk assessment if KILL_CANDIDATE>"
}
Always assess rollback risk before recommending KILL_CANDIDATE:
a session with a long-running open transaction is dangerous to kill.
"""

def triage_tempdb_memory(snapshot: dict, llm_client) -> dict:
    response = llm_client.chat.completions.create(
        model="gpt-4o",
        response_format={"type": "json_object"},
        temperature=0.1,
        messages=[
            {"role": "system", "content": TEMPDB_TRIAGE_PROMPT},
            {"role": "user", "content": json.dumps(snapshot, indent=2)},
        ],
    )
    return json.loads(response.choices[0].message.content)

What AI May Recommend vs What Needs a Human

Tempdb and memory incidents tempt over-automation because the "fix" often looks like killing a session. The gate map must be explicit:

AI may recommend (auto-execute at high confidence): identifying and reporting the culprit session with evidence; alerting with the full diagnostic bundle; updating statistics on a spill-heavy table (reversible, low risk).

AI recommends, human approves: killing a session — always, because rollback risk assessment is probabilistic and a wrong kill during a large transaction extends the outage by the rollback duration. The approval request must include the kill safety note: transaction age, estimated rollback cost.

Human decides, AI advises: adding memory, changing max server memory, adding tempdb data files, changing MAXDOP, enabling memory-optimized tempdb metadata. These are capacity and architecture decisions with cost and risk profiles the model cannot own.

This tiering is the difference between AI-assisted diagnosis and AI-caused outages. The model's job is to compress hours of correlation into minutes and hand the human a decision-ready brief — not to make the irreversible call itself.

---

Building the Training Set from Your Incident History

None of this works without labeled data, and you already have most of it. Mine your ticketing system for past tempdb and memory incidents: for each, reconstruct the telemetry snapshot nearest the incident start (from your metrics history tables), and attach the root cause from the postmortem or resolution notes. Even 30–50 labeled incidents produce a classifier that beats generic heuristics, because it learns your workload's signatures.

Operationalize it with a feature store: a scheduled job captures the wait vector, tempdb breakdown, and memory counters every 5 minutes into history tables. When an incident occurs, the labeling step is just "attach incident_id and root cause to these rows." Over a year, this becomes the highest-value dataset your DBA team owns — it trains the classifier, feeds the anomaly detector's baselines, and supplies golden cases for the Chapter 31 eval harness.

Start before you need it: deploy the snapshot collector now, even before building any model. Six months of unlabeled telemetry becomes labeled the moment you have your next incident to anchor it to.

---

Key Takeaways

  • → **Treat wait deltas as classifier features, not just symptoms.** Normalized wait vectors map cleanly onto root-cause classes — memory grant pressure, allocation contention, version-store bloat, spill pressure — at 85%+ accuracy on labeled incident data.
  • → **Anomaly detection beats static thresholds for memory because it learns rhythm.** PLE cratering at 1 AM during ETL is normal; the same crater at 10 AM is a page — only a model trained on your history knows the difference.
  • → **Good triage names the mechanism and the culprit, with rollback risk assessed.** "Version-store bloat pinned by transaction 98412" plus a kill safety note is a decision-ready brief; "tempdb pressure detected" is noise.
  • → **Kills always need human approval; capacity decisions stay human entirely.** AI compresses hours of correlation into minutes, but rollback risk and architecture calls are judgments the model must not own.
  • → **Deploy the snapshot collector before you build any model.** Six months of wait vectors and tempdb breakdowns become a training set the moment your next incident gives you labels to attach.
